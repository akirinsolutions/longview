# Housing and the Arithmetic of Leverage
~ field: Housing
~ summary: The house is the largest asset most people will ever own, bought with other people's money — which makes it both the middle class's greatest wealth engine and its greatest risk.

## The house is a different animal

A home is not a portfolio asset; it is a **consumption good you
happen to own**, and it behaves unlike anything else in a personal
balance sheet. You cannot diversify it (you cannot own a quarter of
it in one city and a quarter in another without living in two
places); you cannot liquidate it quickly (a house takes months to
sell, at a price the market sets on a slow clock); and you must
*use* it while you hold it (it is the roof, not the position).

The three properties together make housing the one asset in this
corpus that the investing note's toolkit does not reach. The index
the equities note builds its diversification on cannot hold a
street; the portfolio's rebalancing rule cannot move a kitchen
without a move; the behavior note's written plan — the if-then that
survives the storm state — is written for positions you can sell on
a screen, not for the address you sleep at. Every tool of this
corpus, applied to a house, needs a translation, and this note is
the translation.

And yet it is the **largest single asset** on the balance sheet of
most households in most countries — typically three to six times
the entire financial savings, and bought, in the modern era, with a
lump of **other people's money** equal to three to five times the
family's own. The wealth note's decomposition puts housing at a
third to a half of all household wealth, the single largest slice,
and the one no accounting course teaches first. No other asset in
history has been purchased so largely on credit, by people who are
not professional investors.

That combination — illiquid, concentrated, leveraged, essential to
life — is why housing sits at the center of every wealth argument,
every central bank decision, and every crash since 1929, and why
this note reads the house through the four machines the corpus has
already built: the mortgage is the bonds note's promise at
household scale, the lender is the banking note's two-end machine
at street scale, the price cycle is the investing note's weather on
a slow clock, and the land underneath is the money note's claim on
the future. The house is the corpus's one asset that is also a
life, and the rest of the note is about what that means.

## The mortgage: the lever, explained

A mortgage is a **loan whose collateral is the house itself, secured
for decades, and whose payments are fixed** (in the fixed-rate
world) or floating (in the variable one). Stated in the corpus's
vocabulary, a mortgage is a bond held by a bank: the household is
the issuer, the bank is the holder, the fixed payment is the
coupon, the house is the collateral, and the thirty years is the
duration — one of the longest-duration objects in the financial
system, the pension note's stream at household scale, run in
reverse: the household receives the cash now and pays the stream
later, the private person's version of the state's own machine.

Its arithmetic, stated once, explains most of what follows:

- Put 20% down on a house worth 100, and you control 100 with 20.
- The house rises 10%: your 20 becomes 28 — a **40% return on your
  money**. The house falls 10%: your 20 becomes 12 — a **40% loss**.
- The lever multiplies both directions equally, in percentage
  terms. This is the entire mechanism, and there is nothing else in
  it.

Three consequences follow, and each has a name in the history of
housing markets:

1. **Leverage amplifies returns and ruin symmetrically** — the
   percentage story above. A 20%-down buyer rides every move of
   the house at five times the gain or the loss. The investing
   note's survival number — the allocation that survives the worst
   year — is the portfolio's answer to the same math, at five
   times the size, in an asset you cannot sell on the way down.
2. **The margin of safety is the gap between the price and the
   forced-sale price** — because the lender's remedy is the house.
   If the house falls 25% from where you bought it, your 20%
   equity is zero, and you are **underwater**: the house is worth
   less than the loan, so you *cannot leave* — you cannot sell
   without losing the difference, and you cannot walk away without
   the bank taking the asset and your credit. Underwater owners are
   the fuel of every housing bust, because they hold onto losing
   houses, which keeps the supply of houses from clearing the
   price: the lever, past a point, stops being a tool and becomes
   a trap with a mortgage payment.
3. **The loan is fixed, the income is not** — the payment does not
   fall when the house falls, and it does not rise when the job
   falls. The structural risk of a mortgage is not the house; it is
   the *mismatch between a decades-long fixed obligation and a
   decades-long uncertain income*. Every housing stress in history
   has been, at the household level, a story of incomes or rates
   moving against a fixed payment — the bonds note's duration
   problem, lived in a kitchen: the promise was written for a
   career that the labor market did not keep.

> You do not lose money on a house the way you lose it on a share.
> You lose it the way a sailor loses a ship: slowly, while the
> lever does the arithmetic for you, until the day the gap closes
> to zero.
## The mortgage's other end: the bank

The same instrument, read from the other side of the counter, is
the banking note's machine at its most retail: the short end funds
the long end. The household borrows for thirty years; the bank that
holds the mortgage is carrying a thirty-year stream of fixed
payments, funded, as the banking note describes, by deposits that
can leave tomorrow. The mortgage is the longest-duration object on
the retail side of the banking system, and the 2022 repricing — the
episode the bonds note and the banking note both walked through —
showed what that does: when rates rose, the *paper* value of
existing mortgage books fell by a quarter, and the institutions
that could hold the paper to maturity marked it and healed, while
the institutions that had sold it into a market that stopped buying
lived the mark.

The history of housing finance is the history of the bank's
attempts to solve that duration mismatch, and the three attempts
explain the three architectures the modern household can meet:

- **Hold it** — the classical form: the bank keeps the mortgage to
  maturity and lives with the duration. It works in calm rate
  years and becomes a mark-to-market wound the first time the curve
  bends hard, because the fixed stream the bank holds is a stream
  whose price falls when the curve rises, and the bank's equity is
  the gap.
- **Sell it** — the securitization: the mortgages are pooled into a
  tradable stream, the pool is sold to investors, and the bank
  takes the cash and lends again. The pool is the markets note's
  index applied to houses — the insurance note's law of large
  numbers run over *bills* instead of *lives* — and the 2008
  episode is that note's tail bullet, priced from the side: the
  pool that broke broke because the correlation was in the
  *world*, the assets and the insured losses hit together in the
  same week, and no one had put the correlation in a price.
- **Hedge it** — the swap: the bank keeps the mortgage and swaps
  its fixed receipt for a floating one, passing the rate risk to
  the other side of a swap desk. The hedge works until the desk
  that sold it must also carry the 2022-style weather on its other
  side of the balance sheet, and the banking note's two
  equilibria — the quiet institution and the loud one, separated
  only by the mark — decide which version of the bank the street is
  really dealing with.

The graduate lesson, which the household version of the story never
states: **when you sign a mortgage, the counterparty is not a
bank, it is a duration position**. The calm years teach the market
to forget that the lender's asset is a promise paid at the long
end, funded at the short end; the hard year is when the lender
remembers, and the remembering shows up at the kitchen table as
tightened standards, a paused refinance, or a foreclosed street.
The mortgage is a household object whose weather is set by the
banking system, and the two ends of it are never in the same room.
## Why house prices rise: the demand side

The long-run rise of housing in advanced economies is not one
mystery; it is four forces stacked, and the honest analysis keeps
them separate:

- **Demographics and incomes** — the base. More people, higher
  incomes, and (crucially) more *households* per person as families
  fragment; a rising median income bids for a finite street. This
  is the force the wealth note's scale hides in its averages: the
  same median wealth in two cities can sit on two very different
  house prices, because the median household in the constrained
  city is bidding against a wall the other is not.
- **The land constraint** — the supply wall. In the places that
  matter, the houses are on the land, and the land cannot be
  imported. A city that cannot build to its demand is a city whose
  prices track income with a multiplier. This is why geography
  explains more of house-price levels than any other single factor:
  the constrained cities rise, and the unconstrained ones don't.
  The land value is the wealth note's quiet majority — the
  incumbents' wealth, held in a location — and the politics of the
  policy section live in exactly this wall.
- **The credit expansion** — the fuel. Every housing boom in the
  last century has been a *lending* boom first: lower standards,
  higher loan-to-value, more buyers eligible, more money chasing
  the same streets. The prices that credit builds are the prices
  that credit unwinds, and the money note's warning about the
  credit side of a boom applies with a multiplier, because the
  credit is not buying a slice of an index, it is buying the whole
  street.
- **The supply response** — the lag. Building takes years; by the
  time the high prices have produced new supply, the boom that
  ordered it has turned, and the new supply arrives into a slump.
  The housing market's built-in oscillation is this lag, repeated.

The interaction is the lesson: **a demand story that meets a
supply wall and a credit expansion is a boom**, and a boom's end
is usually the credit, not the demand — the incomes that supported
the prices are still there, but the *eligible buyers* the credit
created are not. The four forces are the four answers the question
'why do houses rise' deserves, and the next sections read the fall
and the policy off the same stack, because a machine that has one
way to start has one way to stop.

## The house as the inflation hedge

Because the house is wealth's largest slice, it has earned an
inflation reputation: in the century before the modern central
banks, when the money note's inflation was a normal weather and not
a managed one, the house was the standard hedge — the physical
asset you hold, the rent you collect, the roof that is not a note.
The reputation is not wrong, and it is not safe either, and the
graduate version separates the three claims it carries.

- **The price claim** — in inflation, house prices rise with it,
  because the prices are paid in the same depreciating money, and
  the nominal rise is mostly a *denomination* effect: the money
  note's real/nominal distinction, applied to a roof. A house that
  'keeps up with inflation' in the price is not a hedge that has
  worked; it is an asset that has not lost its purchasing power,
  which is the bare floor, and the floor is not the return.
- **The rent claim** — the flow, not the stock, is the actual
  hedge: the rent stream is a claim on the local economy's ability
  to pay, and in inflation the rents re-price to the new money
  faster than most financial assets do, because the tenant's
  alternative — renting elsewhere, or the payment on a fixed
  mortgage — moves with the same inflation. This is the house's
  real inflation service, and it belongs to the *owner-occupier as
  landlord of the household*, a role most households never write
  down.
- **The debt claim** — the one the lever section made visible:
  because the house is bought with a fixed payment, inflation
  *helps the borrower* — the fixed payment is repaid in money that
  buys less each year, and the real debt deflates. The household
  that is long the house and short a fixed mortgage holds the
  inflation hedge on both ends at once, and the same household
  holds the deflation exposure on both ends in a disinflation: the
  fixed payment, in real terms, *rises*. The 2022–23 episode — the
  money note's repricing at household scale — was exactly this:
  the rate the payment was set against moved, and the lever turned
  the move into a payment.

The synthesis, which the popularity of the 'house as savings' idea
gets backwards: **the house is an inflation hedge only while you
can carry the payment**, and the two claims that matter in a
household budget — the service of the roof, and the debt of the
roof — move in the *same* inflation, in the *same* months, in the
*same* fixed currency. An asset whose hedge and its cost share a
weather is a hedge with a tail, and the tail is the one the next
section prices.
## Why they fall: the unwind

The bust is the same machine run backward, and the sequence has
repeated so often it has a shape:

1. **The trigger** — rates rise, or credit standards tighten, or a
   cohort of incomes wobbles; the marginal buyer disappears. In the
   2022–23 version the trigger was the money note's curve: the
   fastest rate rises in forty years, arriving at the exact moment
   the credit that had built the prices was already thin.
2. **The price correction begins** — the houses that would have
   sold at the top stop selling; prices drift down to find the
   thinner bid.
3. **The lever does its work** — the 20%-down buyers' equity
   erodes; the ones who bought at the peak with 5% or 10% down go
   underwater first. The lever section's trap, now populated.
4. **The forced sellers** — the underwater owners, the jobless, the
   separated; their sales hit the market at whatever price clears,
   pulling the whole street's prices down with them, because a
   street is priced by its weakest sale.
5. **The lender feedback** — falling collateral values make
   lenders withdraw credit from everyone, including the solvent;
   the financing that the *next* buyer needed disappears, and the
   market's liquidity dries up exactly when it is needed. This is
   the banking note's two-equilibrium turn, played on a street:
   the quiet institution and the loud one, separated only by the
   mark, and the mark is the street's price.
6. **The bottom** — arrives when the forced sellers are exhausted
   and the equity of the remaining owners is again large enough to
   hold. It is typically a year or more after the trigger, because
   steps 3–5 take time, and the time is the lever's: equity
   rebuilds at the speed of the house, not at the speed of the
   fear.

The asymmetry people forget: **the fall is usually as fast as the
rise was, and often faster**, because step 5 is a feedback loop
and feedback loops do not respect how slowly the prices went up.
And the recovery is *slower* than the fall, because it must
rebuild the two things the bust destroyed — credit willingness and
household equity — both of which take years, and the credit
willingness is the harder, because it is a belief the banking
system held, and the banking note's lesson is that beliefs rebuild
slower than prices do.

> The bust is not a fall in house prices. The fall is the symptom.
> The bust is a fall in the *willingness to lend against houses*,
> and the prices are what the lever does to a street once the
> lending stops.

## The cycle: the supply lag, made precise

A market usually finds its price quickly, because it is a market of
many similar objects, each a substitute for the next — the markets
note's index, the equities note's company, all of it built on the
assumption that the objects are interchangeable and the sellers are
numerous. The housing market is the opposite machine: a market of
*many local markets of one*, and the oscillation of it comes from
the two properties the previous sections have already used, turned
into a clock.

The clock, stated as a cycle:

1. **Prices rise** for the demand-side reasons — incomes,
   constraint, credit. The rise is slow, because each street
   prices itself off the last sale, and the last sale was set in
   calmer weather.
2. **The rise orders construction**, but construction takes years
   to arrive: the land, the permits, the building, the street. The
   order is real and the supply is not yet there.
3. **The credit that funded the rise tightens** — the central
   bank's inflation job, the banking system's risk appetite, or the
   standards; the marginal buyer leaves, and the prices turn, in
   step with the credit.
4. **The construction arrives into the turn**: the new supply,
   ordered by the boom, lands in the slump, and the slump
   deepens, because the new houses are the most willing sellers in
   a market with the fewest buyers.
5. **The prices fall to the level the surviving demand can hold**,
   the new supply's costs set the floor (no builder undercuts cost
   for long), and the cycle, having taken the full length of the
   construction lag, begins again.

The episodes are the same machine in three weathers: 1929, when
the first global credit unwind met the first global depression;
the 1990s, when two of the machines — the Japanese land price and
the Scandinavian bank systems — turned at the same time, and the
banking note's institutional section learned its two-equilibrium
lesson from them; and 2008, when the securitized pool — this
note's sell-it architecture, the banking note's mark, the
insurance note's correlation — broke the way the unwind section
described. The dates differ; the sequence does not, because the
sequence is *physics*: a lag between the signal and the supply, a
lever between the price and the equity, a feedback between the
collateral and the credit. Three clocks, one period.

And the local property matters as much as the period: a house is
not a share, and a street is not an index. The equities note's
diversification is built on the assumption that a portfolio of
companies is a portfolio of *claims*, while the house is a claim
on a *place* — the place's jobs, the place's schools, the place's
supply wall, the place's weather. Two houses of equal value in two
different places are not the same asset, and the household that
owns one holds a *position in a single city* that no amount of
financial portfolio can hedge, because the hedge does not exist in
a market of one. This is the deep reason the wealth note's
averages are misleading: the average house price conceals the fact
that no two houses are in the same market, and the household's one
street is its one non-fungible bet.
## The policy levers, and what each one actually does

Housing is where economics meets politics most directly, because
the asset is everyone's: the worker, the saver, the landlord, the
builder, the bank, and the state all hold some slice of the same
street, and every lever moves all of them at once. The standard
levers, honestly assessed:

- **Credit standards** (loan-to-value caps, income tests) — the
  most effective *stability* tool: they cap how much of the boom is
  leverage, and therefore how deep the bust can be, because the
  lever section's arithmetic says the depth of the bust is the
  depth of the equity. They are also the least popular, because
  they restrict the exact behavior the boom made profitable, and
  the zoning bullet shows why popularity is not the test.
- **Zoning and supply** — the most effective *affordability* tool
  in the constrained cities, and the most politically loaded,
  because building in an expensive suburb redistributes the
  suburb's land value from its incumbents to everyone else: the
  wealth note's land value, moved by a vote. This is the
  redistribution that the land constraint made possible, and it is
  why the supply wall is a *political* object, not an engineering
  one: the land is held, and the holders vote.
- **Rates** — the central bank's lever, which moves *all*
  borrowing and therefore housing demand as a side effect of its
  inflation job. Rate policy is not a housing policy; it is a macro
  policy with a housing transmission, and the two objectives
  conflict in both directions (cut to save jobs, and houses rise;
  raise to cool houses, and jobs pay the price). The 2022–23
  episode is the conflict in its sharpest form: the money note's
  inflation required the rise, and the lever turned the rise into
  a payment, and the politics of the rate became the politics of
  the household.
- **Demand taxes and subsidies** — the most visible and, in the
  evidence, the least reliable: they move the *timing* of
  transactions more than the level of prices, and each one creates
  a class of winners and losers that entrenches the next cycle,
  because the class that wins the subsidy holds the house the next
  class cannot buy.

The through-line, which is the whole section in one sentence:
**the levers that cap leverage prevent the worst of the bust; the
levers that raise supply prevent the boom from being a price story
in the first place.** Everything else is cosmetic, which is why
housing policy is mostly cosmetic, and which is also why the
household-level decisions of the next section are the only levers
the household itself holds.

## The national scale: land, wealth, and the balance sheet

Zoom out from the street and the house becomes a national object,
and the national object has a size the personal sections cannot
feel: the wealth note's decomposition, run at the scale of a
nation, puts residential housing at the largest single item in the
national net worth — a sum the money note would describe, in its
scale section, as measured not in a year's output but in *several
years'* of it. When a country's house prices move ten percent, the
movement is, in the national accounts, a number the size of a
quarter-year of output, and the reason the central bank, the
treasury, and the legislature all watch the street is that the
street is the balance sheet.

The national machine, read through the corpus, has two objects and
one relation:

- **The land as a claim on the future** — the bonds note's logic,
  at the scale of a nation: the value of the land is the present
  value of the rents the land can collect, discounted at the rate
  the money note sets, and the rent is the local economy's payment
  for the right to stand on the place. The land, then, is the
  nation's own stream of promises, run in the pension note's
  direction — a service paid by the economy to the holder of the
  location — and the 2022 repricing moved it the way it moved
  every other stream: the discount rate up, the present value down,
  the land's mark down, in a year when nothing about the land
  itself had moved.
- **The household debt as the nation's other claim** — the
  mortgages, pooled, are the nation's largest stream of fixed
  promises, and the relation between the two objects is the
  lever's national form: the nation is long the land and short the
  fixed debt, and in an inflation the land rises and the debt
  deflates, while in a disinflation the two move against the
  household at once, which is the national scale of the debt
  claim from the inflation section.

And the crash, at this scale, is not a price event, it is a
*balance-sheet event*: the wealth note's largest item is re-marked,
the household sector's net worth moves by a fraction of the output
of a year, and the banking note's collateral — the same mortgages,
now marked down — moves the lending, the lending moves the jobs,
and the jobs move the incomes that the demand-side section priced
the houses against. The national machine has the same feedback the
street had, one level up, and the reason the state's levers are
mostly cosmetic is that the state does not hold the machine's two
real levers, the credit standards and the supply wall, in the same
hand: the credit is the bank's, the supply is the incumbents', and
the state is the party that raises the rate the other two parties
hate.
## The personal decision, plainly

Stripped to the kitchen-table question — *should I buy?* — the
arithmetic says the answer is not a yes or a no but a set of
conditions, and the conditions are the corpus's own defenses,
applied to the one asset they cannot diversify:

1. **The income test** — the payment, at today's rate, must fit
   the income *with the income's worst plausible year in it*, not
   its best. The mortgage survives on the bad year, and the
   investing note's survival number is the same test at portfolio
   scale: the allocation that survives the worst year, here the
   payment that survives the worst income.
2. **The rate exposure** — know, in writing, what your payment is
   if rates double from here, and whether you are fixed or
   variable. The 2022–23 repricing was a tuition fee paid by
   everyone who had not done this arithmetic, and the money note's
   warning applies: the rate is the one input the household cannot
   choose, and the fixed payment is the one output the household
   cannot move.
3. **The horizon** — the transaction costs of a house (buying,
   selling, the slow market) mean the purchase only pays if you
   intend to hold through at least one full wobble. A five-year
   buyer is not a house buyer; they are a renter with a mortgage,
   and the rent — the consumption cost the buy-versus-rent
   argument usually hides — is the one cost the renter does not
   lever.
4. **The leverage ceiling** — the lower the down payment, the more
   of your financial life is now a position in one street. A 10%
   down purchase makes the family's net worth a 10x lever on local
   housing, and the family's stress tolerance must be sized to
   that: the behavior note's storm state, with the house in it, is
   a storm state the screen cannot show, because the asset will
   not sell to calm you down.
5. **The exit honesty** — not "what will it be worth in ten
   years" but "what do I do, and what can I afford, if it is 20%
   lower in three?" The question that has sunk the most households
   is the one they were not willing to answer, and the written
   plan's if-then, the behavior note's last defense, exists for
   exactly the hour this question lands in.

The house is the one asset where the *use* and the *investment*
are the same object, and the two purposes pull in opposite
directions: the use wants the home you love in the street you
need; the investment wants the constraint, the growth, and the
exit. The mature answer, which is the whole section in one line,
is to buy for the use, price for the risk, and never let the
second purpose — the one the market will be loudest about — set
the terms of the first.

## The deep lesson

Strip the vocabulary away and the note compresses to one fact,
one relation, and one inheritance.

The fact: **a house is a promise made in physical form**. The
mortgage is the bonds note's fixed stream, the bank is the banking
note's two-end machine, the land is the money note's claim on the
future, and the street is the markets note's one market that
cannot be indexed — and the four objects are the four machines of
the corpus, all of them pointed at the one address the household
sleeps at. No other asset in the corpus is *also a life*, and no
other asset in the corpus has ever been bought so largely with
other people's money, by people who are not professionals, for a
use they cannot sell.

The relation: **the lever is the whole of the machine's risk**,
and the lever is the one object in the household's power. The
price of the house is set by the street; the weather is set by the
curve; the cycle is set by the construction lag; the state's
levers are set by the politics. The one input the household
chooses — how much of the house is the household's own, and how
much is the bank's — is the input that decides whether the
street's weather is the household's weather, and the arithmetic is
the lever section's, stated once and never to be forgotten: the
gap between the price and the forced-sale price is the margin of
safety, and the margin of safety is the only thing in the machine
that the household owns outright.

The inheritance, which closes the note and hands it back to the
reader: the house is the corpus's most personal asset and its most
public one — personal, because it is the roof, and public, because
the land under it is the nation's balance sheet, the bank's
collateral, and the state's leverage over the household at once.
The machines of the corpus have a common answer to their common
weather, and the answer is the same one every section of this note
arrived at from its own direction: **the fixed thing is the
promise, the moving thing is the price, and the household's one
job is to keep the promise affordable in the year the price is
hard.** Hold that, and the street is a place to live; forget it,
and the street is a position.

> The market will tell you what the house is worth. The street
> will not tell you what the house is for. The first number is a
> price, moving. The second is a promise, made. The plan is to
> buy the second at the first, and to keep the first from ever
> setting the terms of the second.
