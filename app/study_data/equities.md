# Shares and What a Company Is Worth
~ field: Equities
~ summary: A share is a claim on a stream of future money. Valuation is the art of discounting that stream — and of knowing when the market has priced in hope.

## What you actually own

Buying a share of a company buys you a tiny, undivided slice of the
company itself — not a slice of its *assets*, not a slice of its
*brand*, but a **claim on whatever money the company generates from
now on, after it has paid its debts and its operating costs**. That
distinction is the whole game. You did not buy the factory. You
bought the right, in perpetuity, to a proportion of the cash the
factory produces, minus what the factory needs to keep existing.

A share has two components, and they behave very differently.

1. **The income component** — dividends, when the company pays them.
   This is the cash a share can give you while you hold it. Many
   companies pay them; many do not, preferring to reinvest; the ones
   that skip dividends are betting that reinvestment beats a payout.
2. **The growth component** — the rise in the share price itself,
   which in a healthy market should track the rise in the company's
   money-making power. Over decades, for the average company, growth
   has done far more for shareholders than dividends; over any five
   years, either can do the other thing, and that is why both matter.

The piece people forget belongs in this section, because it explains
why the claim is priced the way it is: you also buy a
**veto-shaped voice** (one vote per share at general meetings, which
for a small holder is symbolic) and a **residual claim** — if the
company ever fails, you are paid only after every creditor, every
supplier, every employee, and every bondholder has been paid in full.
That order of payment is why shares are called *equity*: you are at
the very bottom of the pecking order, and that is precisely why you
are paid for taking the position. The bondholder (the bonds note)
sits above you with a fixed schedule; the supplier sits above both
with an invoice; only the shareholder is left with what is left,
which in the good years is everything, and in the bad years is
nothing. The bottom of the queue is the source of the return, and it
is also the source of the risk: **the claim is residual, so the
claim is leveraged to everything that happens to the business.**

One more structural fact separates the share from the bond, and it
is the one the rest of this note keeps circling back to: **the
claim has no end date.** A bond's schedule terminates; the share's
runs until the company stops, which a healthy company never does.
The claim is therefore an *indefinitely* long object, and an
indefinitely long object is, in the bonds note's vocabulary, the
longest-duration thing in the financial system: its payments are
all, eventually, far enough away that the discount rate's weather
reaches them all. The share is what a bond would be if the bond had
no maturity — which is why the two assets, which look like
opposites, turn out to be the same instrument at two points on one
axis, and why the next section's machinery applies to both.

## From cash flows to a price: the discounting idea

The core of valuation is one sentence: **an asset is worth the
present value of the money it will hand you in the future.** Nothing
more, but everything follows.

A sum of money in ten years' time is worth less than the same sum
today, for a reason that is not subtle: today's money can be put to
work. If you can earn four percent a year safely, then a sum of a
hundred today, invested, becomes a hundred and four a year from
now — and the two states are, economically, *the same thing*, which
is the whole idea in one example. The rate that makes today's money
and tomorrow's money interchangeable is the **discount rate**, and
valuation is the mechanical process of pulling every future payment
back to today at that rate. Each future payment, divided by the
accumulation of the rate over the years that separate it from today,
is its present value; the asset is the sum of those present values.
No judgment is in the arithmetic. The judgment is in the inputs.

The cleanest form of the machine is the perpetuity: a stream that
pays a fixed amount, every year, forever. Its value is that amount
*divided by the rate* — a company that will earn a steady hundred a
year, forever, at a five percent discount rate, is worth twenty
hundreds today, and if the rate falls to four percent the very same
company, earning the very same hundred, is worth twenty-five
hundreds. The division is the most important operation in equity
valuation, and it hides the whole of the next two sections: the
value of a stream is the cash *divided by the rate*, so the stream
moves with the rate even when the cash does not, and it moves
*more* the further out the cash sits, because the further-out
payments are divided by more years of the rate.

Where the art enters is in each of the three guesses the machine
needs:

- **How much cash** is not the same as *profit*, which is an
  accounting number full of judgment. What matters is free cash
  flow: the money left after the company has spent what it must to
  keep growing. Companies can be profitable for years while
  bleeding cash, and unprofitable while building cash; the accounts
  tell you about the past's bookkeeping, the cash flow tells you
  about the machine.
- **For how long** is the question of *durable advantage*. A
  company whose customers could walk to a competitor tomorrow has a
  short future; one with a brand, a network, or a regulatory moat
  has a long one. Most of the gap between a great company and an
  ordinary one is here, not in the size of the current profit —
  because the valuation machine pays for the *duration* of the
  stream as much as its size, and duration is where the division by
  the rate does its work.
- **At what rate** is the question of *risk*: how much extra
  return you should demand for the chance that your other two
  guesses are wrong. This is where valuations go wrong most often —
  a five percent and a nine percent discount rate applied to the
  same company give very different prices, and nobody can tell you
  which one is true.

That last point deserves its weight, because the market is a global
machine for guessing those three numbers — how much cash, for how
long, at what rate — and arguing about them in real time. The price
you see is the *consensus* of the guesses, updated daily, and the
inputs are not visible: you see the output and have to work
backwards. When the price moves, one of the three guesses changed,
but the number alone cannot tell you which, and the investor who
attributes every move to the company (when it was the rate) or to
the rate (when it was the company) is doing the one thing the
machine was built to make hard: reading three moving inputs off a
single moving output. The rest of this note is the vocabulary for
doing exactly that.

## The multiple, and the question it hides

In practice, investors rarely run the full discounting machine; they
use **multiples** — the price divided by some number on the
company's accounts. The three workhorses:

- **Price / Earnings (P/E):** what you pay for each current unit of
  profit. A P/E of 20 means the market is saying *this company's
  profits, at this size, will earn back your price in 20 years if
  nothing grows* — or, equivalently, the market expects the profits
  to grow, and the multiple is its compressed bet on how much.
- **Price / Sales:** for companies not yet profitable; it prices
  the *potential* cash flow, so it is the most hopeful of the three,
  and the one that does the most violence in a speculative era,
  because a company with a story and no cash can still be assigned
  a multiple of its revenue.
- **Enterprise Value / EBITDA:** what you pay per unit of operating
  cash, *after* accounting for the company's debt; the version
  bankers use to compare companies as if they were whole, debt and
  all, and the one that survives the debt-structure differences
  that would otherwise make two similar businesses look unrelated.

The trap of multiples is that they are **answers to a question you
never asked**. A P/E of 15 and a P/E of 30 look comparable, but one
might be a slow company with stable cash and the other a fast
company whose growth is about to run out — the multiple cannot show
you the difference, because the multiple *is* the market's answer to
that difference. The discipline is to never read a multiple alone:
always ask what growth, what duration, and what risk it already
contains, because the division has swallowed all three.

There is a second, quieter trap, and it is the one this note's next
section is about: a multiple is a *state*, not a verdict. The level
of the multiple moves with the discount rate — the division in the
perpetuity, in its compressed form — so the same company, doing the
same thing, carries a different multiple in a low-rate era than in
a high-rate one, for reasons that have nothing to do with the
company. The multiple is the market's answer to three questions at
once, and two of the three — the rate and the mood — are not
properties of the business at all.

> A multiple is a price tag, not a verdict. Two companies can carry
> the same multiple for opposite reasons, and the reason is the only
> thing that matters.

## Cycles: why the same number means different things at different times

The same company, the same accounts, a *worse* multiple — and yet
the share is cheaper, in a sense that matters. This is **cycle**,
and it is the single most counterintuitive fact in equity valuation.

In a boom, profits are at their peak, so the P/E looks *low* — and
the low multiple is a warning, because those peak profits are about
to mean-revert, and the market half-knows it: it is quietly
discounting the coming normalization, and the apparently cheap
multiple is, in fact, the price of a company whose profits are about
to shrink. In a slump, profits are depressed, so the P/E looks
*high* — and the high multiple is often a signal of opportunity,
because the depressed earnings are the bottom of the swing, and the
multiple is dividing a good future by a bad present. The classic,
brutal version: buy when the P/E looks expensive, sell when it looks
cheap. The reason the inversion holds is the same division the
perpetuity showed: the price contains the *future* profits, and the
multiple divides them by the *current* ones, so the ratio is a
statement about the gap between now and the normalization, not about
the company's quality.

The same logic, at the macro level, is why **averaging the market**
beats most of the drama. A whole index is a machine for averaging
out the fates of thousands of companies, so its long-run return is
the economy's long-run growth plus the yield on its profits, minus
the erosion of the parts you get wrong. The parts you get wrong are
mostly *timing*: selling in the trough where the multiple looks
terrifying, and buying in the peak where it looks tempting — the
cycle, at the level of the whole market, inviting the investor to
do exactly the brutal version backwards, every cycle, with the
behavior note's shortcuts doing the work. The index does not time
anything. It just accumulates, and the accumulation is the part of
the return that was actually predictable.

The graduate version adds one clause that the popular version
omits: the cycle runs in the *level* of multiples as much as in
individual ones. The market's average multiple is a state variable —
a regime, in the money note's sense — and the regimes last for
years: a low-rate era holds the whole market's multiples up, and a
rate turn holds them down, and the person who measures a single
company against a single historical multiple is measuring it against
a state that may not come back. Cheap and expensive, in this note's
last section, are always relative to the rate environment, because
the division is there, in every multiple, dividing by the rate.

## Interest rates and the multiple: the hidden lever

The division the perpetuity showed — value is the cash divided by
the rate — does its most dramatic work on the part of the stream
that is *far away*, and that is the section most investors never
connect: **a growth company is a long-duration object**, and the
bonds note's machinery applies to it exactly as it applies to a long
bond.

The mechanism, in words: a company whose value depends heavily on
cash flows ten, fifteen, twenty years out — the growth company, the
one whose earnings are compounding and whose payoff is a future that
has not arrived — has, in present-value terms, most of its value
sitting in the distant payments. The distant payments are the ones
the discount rate touches hardest, because every year between now
and the payment is a year of division. When the rate falls, the
distant payments' present values rise *fast*, and the multiple
expands even though the company has done nothing; when the rate
rises, the distant payments' values fall fast, and the multiple
contracts even though the company is still doing everything it was
doing. The multiple, in other words, is the market's compressed
statement of the rate environment — the bonds note's curve,
re-expressed as a price tag on growth.

The 2022 episode made the mechanism visible to the whole system, and
it is the episode both the bonds note and this note are about,
because the same event was both: the fastest rate rises in forty
years re-priced the long bonds (the bonds note) *and* the growth
shares at once (this note), because both are claims whose value
sits far out on the time axis, and the rate is the one weather that
reaches the far end of the time axis. The calm decade, in both
markets at once, had taught the same lesson in reverse: with the
rate near zero and falling, the distant payments were worth
*enormous* present values, and the multiples of the growth class
expanded to heights that no company's current accounts could justify
— until the rate came back, and the division ran the other way, and
the multiples that had looked like quality turned out, partly, to
be the *rate* wearing a quality costume. The parallel is exact, and
it is the one the portfolio builder must hold: **a portfolio heavy
in growth is a portfolio leveraged to the curve.** The "technology"
allocation and the thirty-year bond are, at the level this note
works at, the same position described in two vocabularies, and the
person who owns one without knowing the other owns a rate bet twice.

The practical consequence follows: the rate environment is not a
background condition for equity valuation; it is *one of the three
inputs*, and the input the individual investor cannot set. The
growth story can be true and the position can still lose, because
the division is doing its work on the far-out payments, and the
division is not a bet on the company — it is a bet on the *path of
the rate through the years the growth takes to arrive*. The investor
who buys a growth company in a rising-rate era is buying a company
whose value must outrun the division; the one who buys it in a
falling-rate era is buying a company the division is helping. Same
company. Different arithmetic.

## How companies create the value you buy

The source of a share's rise, over the long run, is not the market's
mood. It is one of three things, in rough order of reliability:

- **Earnings growth** — the company makes more money than it did,
  year on year. Over decades this is the main engine: the reason a
  broad index of companies has compounded is that the companies
  inside it got bigger and more profitable, and the compounding is
  the compounding of *real production* — the machines, the
  software, the services the economy actually bought. The engine is
  unglamorous and it is the reason the investing note's curve is
  where it is.
- **Buybacks** — the company uses cash to retire its own shares,
  shrinking the denominator of every per-share number. A five
  percent annual buyback is worth, to the remaining shareholder, a
  five percent annual dividend in disguise, and arguably more,
  because the shrinking compounds: the next year's buyback retires
  five percent of a smaller number, and the per-share stream rises
  faster than the raw cash. The double engine, in the good companies,
  is growth *plus* shrinkage, and it is the quiet source of much of
  the long-run return that the multiple's weather is not.
- **The multiple itself** — the market's willingness to pay more
  per unit of profit. This is the part that is pure mood and
  interest-rate weather, and it is the part that makes years
  dramatic. Over twenty years it tends to end roughly where it
  started — the division reverts, because the rate reverts — and
  over three years it can do whatever it likes, which is why the
  decade's winner can be the next decade's laggard, and the two
  facts coexist without contradiction.

A useful habit when the market is loud: split any return, in your
head, into those three buckets. Most of the 1990s' exuberance was
bucket three, and the exuberance was, at bottom, a rate and mood
event wearing a growth costume; most of the twentieth century's
wealth creation was bucket one, and it was, at bottom, an economy
of people building things and getting paid for them. The buckets do
not come back in the same order, and the person who can name which
bucket a year is being paid from — and which bucket their own
portfolio is actually invested in — is the person who will not be
surprised by the turn, because the turn, in this note's arithmetic,
is almost always a change of bucket, and the change of bucket is
the division, again, doing its work on the far end of the stream.

## The quality checklist

If you will not own an index and must pick, the questions that
survive contact with a century of history are a short list, and
each of them is the discounting machine applied to one of its three
inputs:

1. **Does the business have a durable advantage?** Ask what would
   happen if the company doubled its price tomorrow. If the answer
   is "we'd lose our customers," the advantage is thin, and the
   stream the valuation is dividing is a short stream, no matter
   how sweet the current cash. The advantages that survive the
   question are the ones the customer cannot walk away from — the
   brand that is the category, the network that is the market, the
   regulation that is the wall — and they are rarer than the
   stories about them.
2. **Does it need constant reinvestment to stand still?** Some
   businesses are treadmills: all their cash goes to maintaining
   existing scale, and the investor's money goes to the treadmill
   too. The good ones are the ones whose cash *accumulates* — where
   a dollar invested in year one is still working in year twenty,
   and the free cash flow is the stream the perpetuity divides. The
   treadmill company can be profitable, admired, and still be a
   claim on a stream that never arrives.
3. **Can you predict it?** The single biggest historical source of
   investor loss is not bad companies; it is *unpredictable* ones
   bought at confident prices. If you cannot sketch, in two
   sentences, how the company makes money and what would have to go
   wrong, you do not understand it, and the share price does not
   care that you are excited. The predictability is what makes the
   stream a stream rather than a story, and it is the input the
   discounting machine needs most, because a stream nobody can
   bound is a stream the division can price at anything.
4. **What is the price, relative to your own answer?**
   Understanding the business and overpaying for it are different
   things. A great company at a price that requires it to be great
   *and* lucky is a bad investment, because the price has already
   divided in a future that is not in the evidence. The price is
   half the decision, and it is the half the checklist above
   cannot see, which is why it is on the list at all.
5. **What is your exit, honestly?** Not *what will it be worth in
   five years* — what happens if it is worth *half* in five years.
   The position you can afford to lose is the position you are
   actually allowed to hold, and the sizing is where the behavior
   note's architecture meets the valuation: the thesis can be right
   and the size can still be the failure.

## What can go wrong with a share

The failure modes, each named, so the checklist above has teeth:

- **The value trap** — a cheap multiple on a company whose
  cheapness is the market *correctly* pricing a long decline. Cheap
  is often a description, not a bargain: the division produced a
  low number because the stream is short, and the investor who sees
  only the low number is reading the output without the inputs, the
  error the discounting section warned about, at its most common.
- **The story stock** — a price built on what the company *will*
  become, where the accounts show it is not there yet. These are
  not automatically fraud, and some of them turn out right, but the
  margin of error is the entire investment: the whole position
  depends on the story arriving, and a story that arrives late is a
  story the price has already stopped waiting for.
- **The leveraged buyer** — a good company, bought with borrowed
  money, by someone whose leverage forces a sale in the exact year
  the company wobbles. The business is fine; the position was not.
  The leveraged holder is, in effect, a shareholder whose claim has
  a maturity date of their own making, and the maturity — the
  margin call, the forced exit — is the bond's failure mode
  imported into the equity, and it is why the good companies that
  "went to zero" for their holders usually went to zero for the
  leverage, not the company.
- **The regime change** — the interest-rate environment that made a
  valuation look sensible flips, and every multiple in that class
  of asset is repriced at once, on the hidden lever of the
  duration section. This is the one you cannot diversify away with
  more companies of the same kind, because the lever acts on the
  *kind* — on the whole class of far-out streams at once — and the
  diversification that works against one company's fate does not
  work against the division.
- **The honest surprise** — nothing wrong with the business, nothing
  wrong with the analysis, and the world does something nobody's
  model contained. This is the mode that exists to remind the
  investor of the order of the sections: the position size, not the
  thesis, decides whether a wrong thesis is survivable, because the
  thesis was right and the world was not, and the only thing that
  separates the survivor from the casualty is the size.

## The long view, plainly

Strip the machinery away and the equity story compresses to a
handful of durable truths. The stock market is a **device for
converting the economy's growth into the wealth of patient
participants**: over long stretches, the average share has returned
the economy's growth plus the yield on corporate profits, and the
variance around that line has been ferocious but not permanent. The
ferocity is the cycle and the hidden lever, working in years of
weather; the line is the machines — the factories, the software,
the services — getting better at producing, year after year, and
the dividend and the growth of the claim paying the person who
stayed.

One structural fact about the device deserves its own sentence,
because it is the part the short-run reader never sees: **the index
is a self-repairing machine.** The companies that fail are sold off
and replaced, the companies that grow take over weight, and the
organism that carries the index's name in one decade is not the
organism that carried it in the last — the index is a *process*, a
continuous selection, and not a list. The holder who stays in the
process rides the selection without doing the picking: the losers
are removed by the market's own arithmetic, and the winners compound
in the index whether or not the holder ever heard of them. It is
the only part of investing that improves with time on its own, and
it is the reason the investing note's whole argument rests on it.

The participants who captured the line shared a few habits, and the
habits are the behavior note's defenses, applied at the scale of a
market: they treated the market as something that happens *to* the
price, not as a thing they were in a contest with; they
diversified broadly, because the individual companies that fail are
unpredictable while the aggregate is not; they paid little
attention to short-run prices, because the line is visible only at
the zoom where the weather is noise; and they understood, when the
machine was screaming, that the machine is not the economy — that
the price is the division, and the division is weather, and the
economy is the machines.

> You do not have to predict the market. You have to survive it.
> Those are very different jobs, and only the second one is
> repeatable.

## The deep lesson

The whole note, compressed: **a share is a claim on a stream that
has no end date, and the claim's value is the stream divided by the
rate, over years that have not happened yet.** The claim is the
business; the division is the weather; the multiple is the two
together, printed as a number; and the price is the number the
crowd is currently agreeing on, with the agreement revised daily, in
a market of ten thousand motives (the markets note), each running
the same division on its own three guesses.

The private person's share of the lesson is the smaller and the
sturdier one. The three inputs cannot all be known, and the crowd's
agreement about them is the price, so the only variable the plan
controls is the *structure*: the breadth of the claim (the index,
the self-repairing machine), the patience of the holding (the arc,
not the year), the size of the position (the survivable bet), and
the honesty of the scoreboard (the benchmark over the full arc, the
behavior note's defense, pointed at the line instead of the
weather). Hold that structure and the division's weather — the
regime, the cycle, the 2022 repricing of the far end of the stream
— passes through the portfolio as weather passes through a
structure built for weather: it moves the number, it does not move
the plan. The machine is not the economy, the price is not the
promise, and the person who has separated the two has done, in one
afternoon of arithmetic, what a century of markets spends its
crashes teaching.
