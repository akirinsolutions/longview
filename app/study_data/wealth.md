# Where the World's Wealth Sits
~ field: Wealth
~ summary: Roughly half a quadrillion dollars of household wealth, held in a handful of asset classes and a few countries — and distributed far less evenly than any average suggests.

## The scale of the thing

The world's households hold, at the end of the 2020s, on the order of
470 trillion dollars of net financial and non-financial wealth — a
number about the size of the global economy measured over *two* years.
It is useful to feel that number, because every proportion in this note
is a slice of it, and the scale explains why the institutions that
manage it are larger than most countries: the largest fund houses on
earth clear, in a good quarter, more than the annual output of a
mid-size state.

The standard decomposition (the economy section of this app renders the
full tree; this note explains what the slices *mean*):

- **Housing** is the single largest piece — roughly a third to a half of
  the total, depending on the count and the year. In most of the world
  the family home is the family balance sheet, and the housing note
  reads that slice at the only scale where it is true: street by street,
  mortgage by mortgage, on a clock the markets cannot see.
- **Financial assets** make up the rest of the bulk: the **bank
  deposits** that sit idle in the system — the banking note's short end,
  the liquid corner of the storehouse; the **life insurance and
  pension** reserves that are the deferred income of working populations
  — the pensions note's promise, marked to the curve; the **investment
  funds** through which much of the middle class holds markets it will
  never visit — the markets note's index, sliced into units; and the
  **shares and bonds** held directly, mostly by the wealthy — the
  equities note's company and the bonds note's stream, in the form the
  other notes of this corpus have spent their lengths explaining.
- **Other** — the residual: private businesses, land not used for
  housing, the long tail of things that are valuable to their owners and
  illiquid to everyone else, and most of it never priced at all.

The first reading to take from the tree is that **the world's wealth is
mostly not in markets**. The share of total wealth that is investable,
tradeable, liquid — stocks, bonds, funds — is a fraction of the whole,
and most of *that* fraction is held by a minority of households. The
market you watch on a screen is a loud, liquid corner of a much quieter,
much larger storehouse, and the second reading is that the *mix* of the
storehouse is the story: the same economy can be mostly roofs or mostly
balances depending on the count, and the two versions behave differently
in the year the curve bends, because roofs fall on a slow clock while
balances fall with the mark. The rest of the note works down from the
scale — to the regions, to the households, to the one family, to the
arithmetic that decides where it all sits.

## A map by geography

Wealth is not spread where people are. The distribution across regions
has a shape that surprises people who expect it to track population:

- **North America** holds a third of the world's household wealth — on
  roughly 5% of its population. The drivers are the mature pension and
  housing markets, two decades of asset appreciation, and a financial
  system that converts savings into counted assets: the pensions note's
  containers, the markets note's funds, the housing note's lever, all
  running at industrial scale in the one region the count was built in.
- **Europe** holds roughly a fifth, on a fifth of the population — a far
  flatter per-capita picture, with the housing-heavy continental
  economies on one side and the more financialized northern ones on the
  other, and the same population can read twice as rich or half as rich
  depending on which of the two institutional worlds you count in.
- **East and Southeast Asia** is the fast-rising quarter: the wealth of
  the region that just finished industrializing, growing faster than any
  other's in the last decade, still a modest slice of the total — the
  money note's scale applied to the future: a region whose *g* is the
  fastest in the count, compounding a base that is only now catching up
  to the regions that started early.
- **The rest of the world** — Africa, South and Latin America, the
  Middle East and India — shares the remaining fifth among billions of
  households, much of it in housing and informal assets that never enter
  a counted market at all, and the closing section of this note is about
  exactly those assets.

Two conclusions follow from the map. First, **per-capita wealth
concentrates in the regions with the deepest financial systems, not the
highest incomes** — a middle-class household in a financialized economy
sits on more counted wealth than a richer one in an economy where wealth
stays in the house and the family, because the counted wealth is the
wealth the system has made *tradeable*, and the depth of the system is
the size of the tradeable. Second, **the count itself is a Western
artifact**: the same family, measured in two different institutional
worlds, can appear twice-removed in the statistics, because what is
*counted* as wealth depends on what the local financial system makes
tradeable.

> A balance sheet is a document a financial system produces about a
> family. Change the system, and the family's "wealth" changes — without
> the family changing.

The geography, then, is not a fact about the ground; it is a fact about
the *ledger*. The land under the two families is the same land; what
differs is whether the local system has written it into a price: a
region's counted wealth is the present value of what its system has
decided to sell, marked at the rate its curve sets, and the 2022
repricing moved the whole map at once, in a year when no one had moved.
## The distribution within a country

The geographic map is the easy half; the **household distribution** is
the one that matters for anyone making a decision. Wealth is
concentrated in a way that no average is built to show, and the standard
picture, in a typical advanced economy, has three bands:

- The **bottom half** of households owns, on average, almost nothing in
  financial assets — a few months of expenses in deposits, and in many
  countries the one house, often with a mortgage on it. Their *net*
  wealth is thin and their *gross* is a house. The deposits are the
  banking note's liquid corner, the house is the housing note's lever,
  and the two together are the whole of the counted sheet: a balance
  sheet with one roof and a cushion, nothing else.
- The **middle** owns the house (heavily mortgaged in younger cohorts,
  increasingly so) and a growing but modest pile of pension and fund
  balances. The middle class, in the last forty years, has been
  **financialized**: its wealth moved from a tool (the home you live in)
  into a portfolio (the fund you check), and with it acquired the
  portfolio's volatility. The pensions note's retreat from the defined
  promise is the engine of the move, the markets note's fund is the
  destination, and the behavior note's mind is the passenger — a mind
  that has just been handed a position in a market it will have to hold
  through storms it did not book.
- The **top** — the top 10%, and then the top 1% and top 0.1% — holds
  the bulk of financial wealth: direct shares, business ownership,
  property portfolios, and the accumulated result of compounding over
  generations. The ratio of wealth held by the top 10% to that held by
  the bottom 50% in a typical advanced economy is not a small number; it
  is a *regime* — a state the arithmetic of the next sections can
  produce without any actor in it doing anything at all.

The bands are not castes; they are the *shape* the distribution has, and
the shape is what the machines of this corpus act on. The median
household and the mean household are different households, and the
per-capita number the geography section carried is an average running
over the shape.

The mechanisms of the concentration are few and worth naming, because
they are policy levers, not nature. Four do the work, and each one,
looked at closely, is a machine this corpus has already built:

1. **Housing leverage** — the middle class borrows to buy the asset that
    appreciates; the gain is theirs, but the *entry price* matters more
    each cycle, and each cycle's entry price is set by the previous
    cycle's winners.
2. **Compounding on scale** — a return of 7% a year doubles money in a
    decade, and it doubles a large number into a *much* larger one.
    Percentage returns are equal; dollar returns compound the
    inequality, because they compound on the base.
3. **The financialization of the middle** — as pensions moved from
    defined promises to fund balances, the middle class's retirement
    became a market position, and market positions in a rising market
    are held by the people who were already in the market.
4. **Inheritance and intergenerational transfer** — the slow, patient
    engine. Wealth passed down compounds across generations in a way
    that earned wealth cannot, and the share of inherited wealth in
    household balances has been *rising* in most advanced economies for
    half a century.

The point is not that the concentration is unfair — that is a judgment
this note does not make — but that it is **structural and
self-reinforcing**, and that anyone reading a per-capita average should
hear the distribution underneath it.
## The four engines, run through the corpus

The list from the last section is four words long and one machine deep,
and running each engine through the note that owns it shows why the
concentration is structural rather than accidental.

1. **Housing leverage** — the housing note's lever, pointed at a whole
    class. The middle borrows to buy the asset that appreciates, and the
    lever section's arithmetic applies to the class the way it applies
    to the household: the equity, not the asset, is what the family
    holds, and the gain on the borrowed part accrues to the holder for
    free. But the *entry price* is the variable the household cannot
    choose, and each cycle's entry price is set by the previous cycle's
    winners — the housing note's demand side, running on its own output.
    The mechanism is a ratchet: the asset that was the class's on-ramp
    becomes, cycle by cycle, an entry price the next cohort bids against
    the last cohort's lever, and the gap is the wedge the housing note's
    cycle grinds out. Leverage made the middle long the house; the cycle
    made the house a position in the middle.
2. **Compounding on scale** — the investing note's engine, read across
    households instead of through years. A return of 7% a year doubles
    money in a decade, and it doubles a large number into a *much*
    larger one, because the engine acts on the *base*: the same
    percentage, applied to a base of one million, makes more per year
    than the same percentage applied to a base of ten thousand will make
    in a lifetime. The curve does no sorting; it compounds whatever it
    is handed, and whatever it is handed is the distribution of the last
    section. The investing note taught that the engine is the whole of
    long-run wealth; the r−g section is the same engine with the input
    variable left in place, running on the base for decades at a time.
3. **The financialization of the middle** — the pensions note's retreat,
    completed by the markets note's product. When the promise moved from
    the provider's balance sheet to the worker's fund balance, the
    middle class's retirement became a market position, and market
    positions in a rising market are held by the people who were already
    in the market — the index does not discriminate, but it *prices* a
    position, and a price is a thing the holder must live with through
    the years that are hard. The middle got liquidity and portability in
    exchange for volatility it had not asked for, and the behavior
    note's storm state is the storm the fund now carries into the
    kitchen.
4. **Inheritance and intergenerational transfer** — the same three
    engines, slowed by a generation. Wealth passed down compounds across
    generations in a way that earned wealth cannot, because the bequest
    arrives as a *base* — the engine's input, pre-loaded — into the same
    financialized system, so the inherited base is counted, marked, and
    leverable in a way the workshop and the plot were not. The share of
    inherited wealth has been *rising* in most advanced economies for
    half a century, and the r−g section shows why: the fuel is the
    wedge, and the bequest is the delivery mechanism.

Four engines, one shape: each one takes the distribution as its *input*
and hands back a larger version of it, and the hand-back is slow enough
that no family feels the ratchet turn — the housing note's lag, the
investing note's patience, the pensions note's forty years, running on
the same base at once.
## The financialization of the middle

The middle band of the distribution is the one that changed most in the
last forty years, and the change has a name: **financialization**. The
middle class's wealth moved from a tool (the home you live in) into a
portfolio (the fund you check), and the move was not a preference, it
was a *reorganization* — the pensions note's retreat from the defined
promise, completed by the banking system's products and the markets
note's index, with the family's lifetime as the unit of construction.

The pipeline, in the order the money actually flows:

- **The wage becomes a deposit.** The salary lands in a bank account —
  the banking note's short end, the liquid corner of the storehouse —
  and from there it is already inside the two-end machine: the cushion
  is the bank's funding, and the banking note's lesson applies at the
  kitchen table, because the cushion is the thing that is called for in
  the week the income stops.
- **The deposit becomes a container.** The employer's plan — the
  pensions note's defined-contribution machine — wraps the savings into
  a pot with a schedule: the contribution is automated, the accumulation
  is the investing note's engine, and the provider carries the fee (the
  cost section's tax, on the *family's* base, back-loaded into the years
  the family will most need it) while its own exposure ends at the
  moment the money is invested.
- **The container becomes a fund.** The markets note's index does the
  holding: the pot is sliced into units of a basket the family will
  never visit, and the index's averaging is the only diversification
  most middle-class wealth will ever have — the insurance note's law of
  large numbers run over companies instead of lives, which removes the
  company and not the market.
- **The fund becomes a position.** At retirement, the pensions note's
  rollover hour: the deposit machine becomes the payout machine, the
  sequence risk of the decumulation section lands on the family, and the
  behavior note's mind — forty years of reading the fund on a screen —
  is asked to make the decisions of its life in the one state the screen
  makes worst, with the fund's fall on it.

What the middle received in exchange for the move is worth stating
plainly, because the popularity of the fund has made the exchange
invisible: **portability, liquidity, a single balance to look at — and
the volatility of the market, with the family's floor inside it.** The
tool you lived in could not fall in a market; the portfolio you check
can, in a week, by a third. The system retired from the risk, and the
middle inherited it — the pensions note's inheritance at the scale of a
class: the institution that used to carry the promise now charges the
fee on the position that carries the risk, and the fee is collected in
the years the position does its worst work. The distribution consequence
the last section named is complete: the middle is long the market,
whether it knew it or not — unhedged, single-position, and marked to the
curve every day through the one screen the family has learned to read.
The next section runs the same move across the generations.

## Inheritance: the slow, patient engine

The fourth engine runs on a clock the other three only touch. The
bequest — the end of the pensions note, the hour one life's stream hands
its base to the next — is the slowest mechanism in this note, and the
one that has done the most quiet work in the last half century, because
the share of inherited wealth in household balances has been *rising* in
most advanced economies for fifty years, and the rise has three causes,
all already on the table.

- **The wedge.** Between the generation that earns and the generation
  that inherits, the base grows at the return on assets, *r*, while the
  incomes of everyone else grow at the economy, *g* — the r−g section's
  arithmetic, running for the forty years it takes a pot to mature. The
  bequest is not the wealth a family earned in its lifetime; it is the
  wealth the wedge compounded *on top of* it, delivered at the end of
  the running.
- **The asset mix.** What gets bequeathed is no longer what was earned.
  The workshop, the plot, and the trade — the uncounted wealth of the
  last section, the wealth of the parents' generation — have given way,
  in the counted world, to the fund, the balance, the mortgaged house:
  assets that are counted, marked, and leverable, and that therefore
  compound at the counted rate rather than at the rate of the work that
  produced them. The same family, in two institutional worlds, leaves
  two different inheritances, and the geography section's artifact runs
  again, one generation deeper.
- **The lengthened life.** The pensions note's mortality table, applied
  to the bequest: the inheriting generation is older when it inherits,
  because the bequeathing generation lives longer, and the inheritance
  arrives closer to the heir's own prime, into a remaining life long
  enough to hold it and compound it again. The table that prices the
  annuity also stretches the inheritance, by a quarter-century at the
  edges of the data.

The family, then, is the smallest unit of the balance sheet this note
has used, and the family line is the corpus's deepest time scale: the
investing note's engine runs through a life, the pensions note's stream
runs across a life, and the inheritance runs across *lives* — the same
compounding, the same wedge, the same financialized base, at the scale
of a century of family. And the engine is not a villain, which is the
reading the note resists: the mechanism that compounds a family line
into a dynasty compounds a patient saver's pot into a retirement, and
the difference between the two outcomes is the *base* — the distribution
section's shape, handed down. The honest version of the mechanism is one
line: **inheritance is the wedge, with a delivery mechanism.** The next
section states the wedge itself, because every argument about the
distribution, the political ones included, is underneath an argument
about it.
## The household balance sheet, piece by piece

Zooming in from countries to one family, the anatomy repeats at the
micro scale. A typical homeowner's balance sheet has the same structure
as the national one, which is not a coincidence — the national numbers
*are* the household numbers, summed:

- **Assets:** the house (the largest line, often 3–6 times the financial
  lines), the pension pot, the deposit, the fund balances, the car and
  the sofa (the statisticians will not count the sofa).
- **Liabilities:** the mortgage first, by far; the credit card; the auto
  loan; the student debt (in the countries where it exists as a
  household line rather than a policy promise).
- **Net wealth** — assets minus liabilities, and the number that
  matters, because it is what can actually be spent, bequeathed, or
  lost.

Read each line in the corpus's vocabulary and the sheet is a portfolio
of the corpus's own machines: the house is the housing note's levered
claim on one street, the mortgage is the banking note's stream running
in reverse (a bond the household issues, secured by the roof), the
pension pot is the pensions note's container (a claim on a provider's
promise, marked to the curve), the deposit is the banking note's short
end (a claim that can leave tomorrow), and the fund balances are the
markets note's index (a claim on a market the family will never visit).
The typical family, in other words, is not *in* the financial system;
the family is a small portfolio *of* it — one street, one provider, one
market, one income — which is the personal form of every structural
claim the note has made so far.

The dynamic version is where the lesson lives. A household's net wealth
is a **sawtooth**: it rises with the two big assets (house and pension,
both market-linked) and with the accumulation of savings, and it
*craters* when the market turns, because the same two assets that built
the balance sheet are the same two that fall. A household that built its
net worth in a bull market of houses and shares has built a net worth
that is, structurally, a *position* in that market — and positions fall
when markets fall, in the bad year, all at once. This is the
household-scale version of the concentration story: the leveraged middle
class is long the market, whether it knew it or not. The behavior note's
defenses, applied here, are the corpus's whole answer in miniature:
write the plan in the calm, point the scoreboard at the floor rather
than the weather, and let the boring lines — the deposit, the schedule,
the roof — carry the life while the loud lines do their falling.

## Why the distribution keeps widening: the arithmetic

The widening is not a mystery; it is the interaction of a slow return
with a slow economy, and the arithmetic is worth stating cleanly,
because it is the same arithmetic that governs a single compounding
account:

- Let wealth grow at rate *r* (the asset returns, say 6–7% a year in a
  typical bull regime) and incomes grow at rate *g* (the economy's real
  growth, say 2%).
- Then the ratio of wealth to income grows at *r − g* — a point or more
  a year — and over decades that gap is a wedge. The wealthy, whose
  wealth grows at *r*, pull away from everyone whose income grows at
  *g*, **even if no one is doing anything wrong at all**.
- The escape valves are the ones that raise *g* for the bottom
  (education, mobility, a broad-based labor share) or that tax the *r*
  side (inheritance, capital gains, the unearned slice of returns).
  Every political argument about wealth is, underneath, a dispute over
  *r − g* and which lever to move.

Run the arithmetic through the corpus and the inputs have owners. *R* is
set by the money note's curve and the markets note's returns — the
discount rate and the risk premium, the two numbers the bonds note and
the equities note both live on — and *g* is set by the economy, the
growth the equities note's index rides. The wedge, then, is not a
household variable; it is a *system* variable, set by the central bank's
inflation job and the market's weather, and applied to a base the
distribution section showed is already unequal. A system variable acting
on an unequal base is a wedge that widens whether any actor intends it
to, and the intent is precisely what makes the wedge political: the
note's neutrality — no one is doing anything wrong — is the thing the
argument is about, because the argument is over the *inputs*, and the
inputs are chosen in the budget and at the central bank, not in the
family's kitchen.

The arithmetic is also the arithmetic of **leverage's asymmetry**: a
leveraged holder of an appreciating asset sees their *equity* grow
faster than the asset, because the borrowed part of the purchase
appreciates for free — the housing note's lever, the investing note's
engine, the same sentence said once more. The same leveraged holder, in
the down year, sees their equity fall *faster* than the asset — and hit
zero before the asset does, which is when the forced selling begins (the
markets note's crash section, at the kitchen-table scale). The forced
selling is the wedge's *downside*: the holder who hits zero is the
holder whose base is now nothing, compounding from zero, while the
holders who kept their base compound from the base. The wedge widens in
the up year and the lever breaks holders in the down year, and the two
together are the whole engine: the concentration is the up year's
arithmetic, plus the down year's casualties.
## The other side of the ledger

Every line on the household sheet has a partner, and the partners are
the section the popular version of the wealth story leaves out, because
a balance sheet has two sides and the wealth story usually tells one.
The national balance sheet, read through the corpus, is the same two-end
machine the banking note built, at the scale of a country:

- **The deposit is the bank's funding.** The household's liquid corner
  is the banking note's short end — money that can leave tomorrow — and
  the banking system exists, in the ledger, *because* the households'
  cushions exist. The wealth on one side of the street is the liability
  on the other, and the banking note's two equilibria, the quiet
  institution and the loud one, are decided by what happens to that
  liability in the hard week.
- **The pension claim is the provider's promise.** The family's pot is
  the pensions note's stream held at the long end, funded at the short
  end, marked to the curve — the promise and the price the pensions note
  separated, and the 2022 repricing moved the price while the promise
  stood still.
- **The house is the mortgage's collateral.** The housing note's
  national scale: the nation is long the roof and short the fixed debt,
  the two objects of the ledger, and the household debt — the mortgages,
  pooled — is the nation's largest stream of fixed promises, the bonds
  note's stream at the scale of a street.
- **The fund unit is the manager's mandate.** The markets note's index,
  from the issuer's side: the family's balance is the fund house's short
  end, and the fee — the cost section's tax — is the price of the whole
  arrangement, collected on the largest base, in the years the base is
  smallest.

The identity the section needs is one line: **the sum of the wealth is
the sum of the liabilities it sits on, plus the assets that have no
partner** — the land, the building, the workshop, the plot, the things
the next section says are not counted at all. Wealth and debt are not
opposites in the ledger; they are *the same fact written twice*, and the
two move together, which is the national form of the sawtooth: in the
boom the marks rise on both sides at once, and the net worth grows even
where nothing real has been produced, and in the bust the housing note's
balance-sheet event plays out — the largest item re-marked, the
collateral of the banking note falling, the lending tightening, the
incomes the demand side priced the houses against wobbling, one level up
from the street. The 2022 episode is the demonstration in the public
record: the curve bent, the marks on the nation's largest lines moved,
and the net worth moved with them, in a year when no one had built or
sold or moved. The wealth of a country, in other words, is a *mark on
the nation's balance sheet*, and the mark is the money note's curve, and
the curve is the thing the whole corpus has been pricing against.

## What the numbers cannot see

The closing map is the honest one, because a map of counted wealth is a
map of *financialized* wealth, and the world is mostly not
financialized:

- **Informal assets** — the workshop, the plot, the family business in
  the village, the remittance flows that are the *real* balance sheet of
  much of the Global South. These are wealth in every sense that matters
  to their owners, and they appear in no global table, because the
  geography section's ledger does not reach them: no market, no mark, no
  count.
- **Human wealth** — the present value of the income a person will still
  earn over a working life, which for a healthy adult is, in most of the
  world, larger than the financial balance sheet. It is not counted
  because it cannot be traded, which is exactly why it is the wealth
  most people actually own: the pensions note's stream, before the
  retirement, the stream the whole corpus is ultimately in service of.
- **The unpriced** — the equity a family holds in a business it will
  never sell, the house in the hometown, the collection. Illiquid wealth
  is wealth, and the liquid count systematically undercounts the
  patient, because the price requires a seller and the patient has none.

The practical consequence for the reader of this note: **the tables of
global wealth are a map of where the tradeable money is, not where the
value is.** Use them to understand the *system* — its centers, its
leverage, its concentration, its wedge — and do not mistake them for a
census of human security. The family that owns a home and a trade and a
generation of skill is wealthy in the way that matters; the system will
simply not count it until someone makes it tradeable, and the day it
does, the geography section's artifact will have run one more time.

> The wealth of the world, as counted, is a balance sheet of the
> financial system. The wealth of the world, as lived, is mostly
> somewhere else.
## The deep lesson

Strip the vocabulary away and the note compresses to one fact, one
relation, and one inheritance.

The fact: **the counted wealth is the wealth a financial system has made
tradeable.** The scale section's 470 trillion dollars is a number the
system's ledger produces; the geography section's map is a map of the
system's *depth*, not of the ground; the distribution section's bands
are bands within what the system counts; and the household sheet is a
document the system produces about a family. Change the system, and the
family's "wealth" changes — without the family changing — and that line
is the whole note, because everything this note has called wealth has,
at the moment of counting, already passed through the tradeable, and the
tradeable is the system's product, not the family's.

The relation: **the wedge and the lever decide where the counted wealth
sits.** The r−g section's wedge sets the drift — the system variable,
chosen at the central bank and priced by the market — and the housing
note's lever sets the amplification — the one dial the household does
hold — and the two run on the same base, the distribution section's
shape. The wedge widens the base; the lever multiplies the moves on the
base; the sawtooth delivers the result to the family's sheet; and the
bequest hands the result to the next family. A machine with a drift, an
amplifier, a delivery, and a delivery across generations is a machine
that produces concentration as its ordinary output, and the output is
what the note has been describing from the scale down to the kitchen
table.

The inheritance, which closes the note and hands it back to the reader,
is the corpus's common fact applied to the map: **the fixed is the
promise, the moving is the price, and the wealth story is the two moving
apart.** The family's home, the family's career, the family's skill, the
family's years are the fixed things; the marks on the sheet, the numbers
on the map, the ratios in the distribution are the moving numbers,
marked to a curve the family did not set, at a rate the family cannot
choose. The note's bequest to its reader is the corpus's: read the map
for what it is — a document the system produced about a life — spend the
life on the fixed things, and let the moving numbers be the weather,
because the weather, unlike the plan, passes.

> The map tells you where the system's money is. It does not tell you
> where your life is. The first number moves with the mark; the second
> is the only thing you have.
