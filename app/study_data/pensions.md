# Pensions, Insurance, and the Long Risks
~ field: Pensions
~ summary: Pensions are promises made today about money owed in forty years. Insurance is the technology for sharing risks that would ruin one person. Both are built on the same deep arithmetic.

## The problem both exist to solve

A human life has a risk profile no other asset class can match: the
risk is **long** (it spans fifty working years and thirty retirement
years), **certain in arrival** (you will age; most people will need
income in their seventies), and **uninsurable by the individual** (no
one can set aside enough, alone, to replace a salary for three
decades without betting the whole effort on a single investment
career run in a market they do not control). Pensions and insurance
are the two institutions built to meet that profile, and they meet
it with the same two tools:

1. **Pooling** — the risk is shared across many people, so that the
   cost of any one person's long tail is spread thin across a
   population that can afford it.
2. **Time** — the money is committed for decades, so the pool can
   afford to hold long, patient assets that a short-horizon investor
   could not: the assets are the bonds note's machinery at the scale
   of a population, and the scale is the point.

Everything in this note — the defined benefit, the defined
contribution, the actuarial math, the funding gap, the annuity, the
national machine — is the application of those two tools to the
specific risks of a life. The rest of the corpus can be read, in
part, as the private person doing the same two things alone: the
investing note's pool is the index, and its time is the arc, the
compounding that only works if it is uninterrupted. The institutions
are the machines; the corpus is the manual for running the machine
yourself, after the institutions retreat.

## The two pension machines

A **defined benefit** pension is a *promise about the future*: the
employer commits, when you join, to pay you in retirement an amount
set by a formula — a fraction of your final salary for each year of
service. The employee's contribution is smaller than the promise;
the employer tops it up. The risk sits with the **promisor**: if the
assets fall short, the employer (or, in the public sector, the
state) owes the difference. The employee has converted a career's
wages, in part, into a **fixed annuity** — the one financial product
whose only job is to outlive its owner, and whose payments are fixed
in exactly the way the bonds note's promise is fixed: known in
amount, known in schedule, exposed to the discount rate and to the
promisor's ability to keep the word.

A **defined contribution** pension is a *container about the
present*: you (and perhaps your employer) pay in a percentage of
salary, it is invested, and whatever the container holds at
retirement is what you get, converted into an income by the rules of
that day. The risk sits with the **employee**: the market's last
decade before your retirement, the inflation of the drawing
years, the sequence of returns in your fifties, the allocation you
chose at twenty-five and forgot — all of it is yours to carry. The
container does not promise; it accumulates, and the accumulation is
the only promise in the machine, a promise about the *process* (the
money will be invested) rather than the *result* (the income will
be there).

The shift from the first machine to the second, across the last
forty years, is one of the defining financial changes of the era,
and it is a **transfer of risk from the institution to the
individual**: the corporation that once absorbed the market risk of
its workforce's retirement now hands the employee a fund and a
spreadsheet. The transfer was rational for the companies, for the
arithmetic reason the bonds note gives: a benefit whose cost moves
with the discount rate is a long-duration liability, and the 2022
repricing showed what a long-duration liability does in a
rising-rate year, to the holder of it. It was not, for the
individuals, a neutral exchange — it moved onto millions of
households a risk those households had neither the horizon nor the
tools to price, and which the investing note's four forces are the
tools for — the transfer was accompanied, in most households, by
the absence of the manual.

> The defined benefit pension is the last mass-scale contract in the
> Western economy that says: we will remember you in forty years.
> Its retreat is a change in the moral architecture, not just the
> arithmetic.

## The defined benefit machine, in its hour of work

The first machine, run to its conclusion, is the cleanest object in
this note, and the graduate version reads it the way the bonds note
reads a bond: **the promise is a stream, and the stream has a price,
and the price moves with the rate even when the promise does not.**

A defined benefit promise, discounted, is a liability with a
duration — the bonds note's measure, applied to a career: a promise
that pays for thirty years of retirement, beginning in twenty years,
has a duration in the decades, and it is one of the
longest-duration objects in the financial system: a stream that
runs to ninety-five has most of its value in payments the market
cannot yet see. The funding question is the bonds note's duration
question at population scale: the assets (mostly long bonds and long
shares, because the liabilities are long) must match the
liability's duration, and the match is a continuous, active job —
both sides keep moving, the promises reprice with the curve, the
assets' prices move in the opposite direction, and the year the
curve bends hard is the year the match breaks visibly.

That is the 2022 episode, at pension scale: the fastest rate rises
in forty years hit the machine on both sides at once — the
*promises* got cheaper on paper, while the *assets* — the long
bonds that had been the match — lost a quarter of their value in
the price. For the machines that could hold the paper to the end,
the episode was a mark that healed; for the machines whose promises
are *political*, it became a budget argument, and the budget
argument is where the actuarial section's arithmetic goes to die in
practice.

The deeper structural fact, which the bonds note's institutional
section stated for banks and which the pension is the standing
example of: **a promise made to many people at once, by one
promisor, who cannot all be paid at once, is a coordination object,
not an asset.** The defined benefit pension is a bank in a coat —
the short end (the salary, the contribution, the tax levied now)
funds the long end (the stream that begins in twenty years), the
calm years teach the market to forget the gap, and the year the
curve moves is the year the gap remembers itself, and the
remembering can become a run on the promisor's *other*
obligations: a company or a state promises many things, and the
news about one promise moves the confidence in all. The banking
note's two-equilibrium logic — the quiet institution and the loud
one, separated only by the mark — is the machine's weather.

## The defined contribution machine, in its hour of work

The second machine, read the same way, is the first machine with the
risk moved to the other side of the counter — and the graduate
version notes what the move actually does to the worker.

The container's arithmetic is the investing note's arithmetic, at
the scale of a career: the contributions compound (the engine), the
allocation sets the survival number (the curve's shape), the fees
are the silent tax (back-loaded, on the largest base, in the final
years — the cost section's point at pension scale), and the
behavior is the weather the plan must survive (the storm state of
the behavior note, with a spreadsheet attached). The machine does
none of these things for the worker. It accumulates, and then it
hands the sum back, and the handing-back is the rollover — the
investing note's rollover, the behavior note's rollover, the hour
where the deposit machine becomes the payout machine and every
shortcut fires at once, concentrated, in the person told, for forty
years, that the container was "their retirement."

The asymmetry of the transfer is now visible in the clean form.
Under the first machine, the worker carried one risk — the
promisor's survival — and the institution carried the market. Under
the second, the worker carries the market, and the institution
carries *nothing at all*: the container provider charges the fee (the
cost section's tax, this time on the *worker's* base) and its
exposure ends when the money is invested. The fee is the
institution's return, and it is back-loaded in exactly the way the
cost section described: the institution's revenue is largest, and
the worker's exposure is largest, in the same years — the rollover
years, when the behavior note says the mind is least able to make
the decisions the rollover requires. The transfer was completed, in
other words, at the moment the transferred risk was most expensive,
and the fee collected the difference — which is the fact the
popular version leaves out, and the one that explains why the
machines that *sell* the second machine were most against the first.

## The actuarial arithmetic

The mathematics underneath both machines is the same as the bonds
note, at life-scale: a pension is a **stream of fixed future
payments**, and its cost is the present value of that stream,
discounted and weighted by the chance each payment is actually made.
Three inputs decide the number:

- **The discount rate** — the assumed return on the assets held to
  pay the promises. The entire funding story lives here, because the
  number is brutally sensitive: a five-percent discount rate prices
  a promise; a seven-percent rate prices the same promise nearly a
  quarter cheaper, for the reason the bonds note gave — the
  division, run over forty years, moves most of the value into the
  distant payments, which are the whole stream. The debates that
  have wrecked public pension budgets are, underneath, arguments
  over one rate.
- **The mortality table** — how long the retirees will live. This is
  the input the actuaries love, because it is the one the
  *employees* get wrong: people consistently underestimate their own
  longevity, and a pension that pays until death is a bet against
  your optimism about dying young. The rising life expectancy is the
  slowest, most certain cost increase any pension carries — a stream
  that gets longer every decade, by a half a year at a time, so
  slowly that no generation feels it, so certainly that every one
  pays for it.
- **The investment risk** — the spread between the assumed return
  and the realized one, compounding over the same forty-year span.
  A pension funded on an assumption the market does not keep is a
  pension in deficit *by assumption*, and the deficit appears,
  eventually, as either higher contributions or smaller promises,
  depending on which side of the counter holds the pen.

The **funding gap** — the difference between the promises' present
value and the assets held — is the standing number in every
pension's news, and the honest reading is a *statement about the
discount rate as much as about the assets*: the same assets and the
same promises show a huge deficit at one rate and a surplus at
another, because the *valuation* moves with the rate even when
nothing real has, and the 2022 episode is the demonstration, in the
public record, of the whole machine turning on the one input.

And the rate is not chosen by a neutral party; the choosing is the
politics. The party that owes the promises benefits from a high
valuation rate: a high rate prices the same stream lower, the lower
price is a smaller deficit, the smaller deficit a smaller
contribution, and the contribution is the number that lands in a
budget, and the budget is where the argument happens. The party that
receives the promises
does not see the rate at all: the worker's future income was agreed
in the formula, and the formula does not re-price when the curve
bends. The structural fact, then, is that **the funding gap is an
input-chosen number, and the party that chooses the input is the
party that owes the money**, and the honest reading of the news,
therefore, runs in three steps: when a pension announces a deficit,
ask which rate produced it, who chose it, and what the promise was,
before the discounting, in the simple currency of the stream — how
many years, of what income, for whom, beginning when.

## The annuity: the product that finishes the job

The pension promise, at retirement, has to become an **income**, and
the instrument that does the conversion is the **annuity**: a
contract in which you hand over a sum and receive a fixed payment
for life, the provider carrying the risk that you live longer than
the money lasts. The annuity is the only product that eliminates
**longevity risk** — the risk of outliving the portfolio, which is
the one risk no diversification and no fee-cut addresses, because it
is a risk about *you* rather than about the market, and the market
is the only risk the rest of the corpus can do something with.
The annuity is a bond the other way, in the bonds note's vocabulary:
the provider pays you a fixed stream, and your life is the
maturity — a stream that ends on a date no one knows, which makes it
the instrument in this corpus whose schedule is written by biology.

The economics of the annuity explain its unpopularity, and the
unpopularity explains the retirement gap:

- The provider prices it on the mortality table, which is the
  *average* person; the people who buy annuities tend to be the
  *cautious* people, who live longer than average, so the price
  embeds an **adverse-selection loading** that makes the headline
  yield look worse than it is for a typical buyer — the pool prices
  the person who asks for the price, and the person who asks is not
  the average person.
- The conversion looks like a *loss* to a mind used to markets:
  handing a hundred to a company in exchange for six a year,
  forever, reads as a bad trade in any year the market returns eight
  percent. It is not a trade in the market at all — it is the
  *removal of the market from the equation*, which is exactly the
  point, and exactly why it feels like surrender: the behavior
  note's mind, which has spent a lifetime reading prices, is asked
  to make a decision the price no longer applies to, and a decision
  that cannot be checked against the screen is a decision the storm
  state cannot make.
- The provider's own funding problem mirrors the pension's: an
  annuity book is a promise paid at the long end, funded at the
  short end, and the annuity provider is a pension run backwards,
  the same two-end machine, the same duration match, the same
  2022-style weather, now by the company on the other side of the
  counter: the floor it offers is the system's risk *relocated*, and
  the honest question is whether that promisor's survival can be
  trusted more than your own portfolio management.

The practical architecture most planners now use is the **hybrid**:
convert the part of the retirement pot that must fund the certain,
unending floor (the spending that will definitely continue — the
rent, the food, the medicine, the life itself) into an annuity or
its public equivalent, and leave the rest as a portfolio for the
uncertain tail, the inheritance motive, and the inflation the fixed
annuity cannot see. The split is a judgment call, but the
*existence* of the question is the lesson: **a portfolio is not a
retirement income; it is a reservoir that must be managed into one,
and the management is the risk** — the decumulation problem, named
in the next section, is the annuity's job done without the product.

## Decumulation: the sequence that decides the drawing years

The rollover — the hour the machine switches from deposit to payout
— is where the *sequence* matters, and the sequence is the one
input the popular version of planning never writes down, because it
cannot be seen in advance.

The mechanics: a portfolio that must pay an income for thirty years
is not a portfolio that is *invested*, it is a portfolio that is
*consumed*, and the order in which the years arrive does the work
the allocation cannot. The same thirty-percent fall does different
damage depending on *when* it arrives. Arrive in the tenth year of
a forty-year accumulation, and the damage is a dent: the engine
has thirty years left on the same base, at the same rate. Arrive in
the first year of the thirty-year payout, and the damage is a *step
change*: the withdrawal is taken from the fallen base, the base has
to regrow, and the regrowth is slower than the withdrawal continues,
so the pot that should have lasted thirty years runs out in twenty:
the same market, a decade earlier, was survived, and is not
survived a decade later. This is **sequence risk**: the
decumulation version of the asymmetry the investing note derived,
run on the payout phase, where the base is being drawn down rather
than built up — the engine runs in reverse, and the shrinking base
compounds the damage, the other way.

The defense is the architecture, and the architecture is the hybrid
section's, made operational: the floor is the annuity (the spending
that will definitely continue is paid by the product that cannot
fall), and the portfolio is sized for the *tail* (the spending that
might arrive — the health costs, the inheritance, the inflation), so
that the market's worst year can hit the tail without touching the
floor, and the step change is removed by construction. The written
version — the behavior note's if-then, at the scale of a retirement:
if the portfolio falls a third in the first year of drawing, the
floor continues, the tail's spending is the part that flexes, and
the portfolio is not sold into the fall, because selling into the
fall is the act that converts the weather into the step change, and
the weather, unlike the sale, passes. The sequence, in other words,
is managed not by predicting it (no one can) but by *positioning
the spending against it*: the certain spending on the promise that
cannot fall, the uncertain on the asset that can, the two separated
before the weather arrives — the calm hour the behavior note says
is the only one the plan is made in.

## Insurance: the same machinery, one life at a time

General and life insurance is pooling and time applied to risks that
are **short and fat-tailed** instead of long and certain — the
accident, the illness, the early death, the house fire, the
business that stops — and the arithmetic is the same machinery at a
different scale:

- **The pool** — many small premiums from many people, paying the
  few large losses that actually occur. The law of large numbers is
  the engine: the more similar lives in the pool, the more the
  actual losses track the expected ones, and the cheaper the
  protection per life — the same averaging that makes the index the
  investing note's pool, run over *lives* instead of companies, and
  the floor is the same floor: the risk that the pool *shares*, the
  risk that hits all the lives at once, which is the next bullet.
- **The pricing** — the premium is the expected loss (the
  probability times the severity, from the data) plus the cost of
  the pool's administration plus a margin for the years when the
  data lies. The data is the product: an insurance company is, in a
  real sense, a statistics business that happens to sell peace of
  mind, and the statistics are the actuarial section's tables, run
  on the short tail instead of the long one.
- **The tail** — the part the price cannot see. The model prices the
  losses it has data on; the *correlated* losses — the flood that
  hits a whole coast, the pandemic that hits a whole industry, the
  market crash that hits a whole pool of insured *assets* at once —
  are the ones that break pools, because pooling cancels
  *independent* risk and concentrates *correlated* risk. Every
  insurance crisis in history is a correlation crisis, and the 2008
  version deserves naming, because it is the version the pension
  world also carries: the insurer whose *assets* are a pool of
  market exposures, and the crash that hits the assets and the
  insured losses *together* is the double blow the model never
  priced — the model priced each side of the balance sheet
  separately, and the correlation was in the *world*, not in either
  side. The pool that breaks is the pool whose risks were not
  independent, and the independence was the assumption the price
  was built on.

The deep symmetry, for the reader: **a pension is a hedge against
living too long; an annuity is a hedge against the same, sold by
the pool; an insurance policy is a hedge against dying or breaking
too soon.** A complete personal risk picture is the two together —
the long tail and the short tail — and most households carry an
inadequate version of at least one, usually because the two are
sold, priced, and understood in completely separate worlds: the
pension sold as a benefit, the insurance sold as a product, the
annuity sold as a yield, and the three pieces of the same risk map
met, in the household that needs them, only in the year the risk
arrived, in the hour the mind is least able to buy what it needs.
The one-page plan the investing note ends with has a second
column, then, and the second column is the short tail: the income
protection for the years the salary could stop, the cover for the
costs that would ruin the household in a year, the life cover for
the dependants the household's *other* promises are for.

## The public machine: the same arithmetic, one nation

The state runs both machines at national scale, and the graduate
version reads the national machine the way this note has read the
private one: the same two tools, pooling and time, at the scale
where the promisor is the tax power and the pool is the population.

The first is the **pay-as-you-go state pension**: the workers of
today pay the retirees of today, and the promise to you, made in
your twenties, is that the workers of *your* retirement will pay
you. The pooling here is not over assets, it is over *time and
demography*: the pool is the ratio of workers to retirees, and the
promise is funded by the labor force's income, not by a portfolio,
which makes the state pension the one "pension" in this note with
*no assets at all* — a claim on future taxes rather than on future
returns, and the two are not the same claim, because the future
taxes depend on the economy and the future returns on the market.
The risk profile of the two machines differs in one quiet and
decisive way: the funded machine's risk is the *market*, which falls
and recovers, and the pay-as-you-go machine's risk is the
*demography*, which does not fall and recover, it simply *ages* —
the workers-to-retirees ratio is a curve with one direction, and a
pay-as-you-go promise's decades are the decades of a dependency
ratio that moves, by the data, toward a future in which each worker
supports more retirees than the promise assumed. The state pension,
in other words, is a bond whose maturity is a *demographic* — a
claim whose value is the labor force of 2060, against a stream (the
taxes) whose length the mortality table, not the market, is
shortening. The reform debates of the last decades — the raising of
the age, the lengthening of the contribution years — are the
machine adjusting its formula, and the adjustment is the only one
available, because the machine cannot be diversified: there is only
one population, and the population that is aging is *yours*.

The second is the **public service pension**, the defined benefit
machine of the first section, with the state in the employer's
chair. It carries the earlier sections at national scale: a stream
with a duration in the decades; assets mostly long bonds, the
duration match, the 2022 repricing; the input-chosen funding gap,
and the party that chooses the input is the party that owes the
money — the same state, in the same budget, arguing about the price
of its own promises — the argument landing in the public record as a
"deficit" or a "surplus" that is, underneath, the curve's weather
in a budget's clothes. And the national machine carries the coordination
fact at its largest: the state is the promisor of many promises at
once — the pensions, the health care, the debt — and the year the
market moves the price of *one* of them moves the confidence in *all
of them*, because they are all claims on the same future taxes: the
implicit and the explicit debt of the state, one stream, priced as
one number.

> The state pension is the country's bond against its own future.
> Every other promise in this note is a contract between two
> parties; this one is a contract between a generation and the one
> that has not arrived yet.

## The private person's picture, after the retreat

The synthesis for the reader, because the institutions' retreat is
the private person's inheritance: the machines this note has
described are the machines the private person now runs *alone* —
the contribution (the investing note's fuel), the allocation (its
survival number), the decumulation (this note's sequence defense),
the annuity (the floor), the insurance (the short tail) — and the
running of them is the whole of what a "retirement plan" means now.

The picture, in the order the years arrive:

1. **The accumulation decades** — the contribution, on a schedule,
   automated, uninterrupted: the investing note's engine, with the
   behavior note's automation as its protection, and the cost
   section's index as its structure. The risk here is the slow leak
   — the fee, the allocation that drifts, the contribution that
   pauses after the bad year — and the defense is the same one the
   whole corpus converges on: the boring things, done on schedule,
   in the days that look like no news.
2. **The decade before the rollover** — the decade in which the
   plan must be *written*, because the rollover is the behavior
   note's largest hour and the written plan is the only thing
   present in it. The allocation for the decumulation years is
   decided in the calm of the fifties (the survival number, the mix
   that can fall a third without touching the floor); the annuity
   question in the same calm (the floor, sized to the certain
   spending); the sequence defense in the same calm (the certain on
   the promise, the uncertain on the asset). The fifties are the
   last calm the financial life has, and the plan written there is
   the plan that will be *lived* in the hour that is not calm.
3. **The rollover and the drawing years** — the sequence section's
   architecture, running: the floor pays, the tail spends, the
   portfolio holds, and the bad year, when it comes — and it comes,
   the weather does not take vacations — hits the tail and not the
   floor, and passes. The behavior note's scoreboard, pointed at the
   floor rather than the tail, is the last defense, the one that
   decides whether the drawing years are a life or a liquidation.
4. **The end** — the bequest, the care costs, the mortality
   crossover at which the survivor's income must come from
   somewhere else entirely, and the annuity's joint form (the
   provider who pays while *either* of two lives lasts) carrying the
   last of the pooling, one household at a time, the law of large
   numbers in its smallest form: two lives, priced on the table, the
   table's average doing its quiet work over the couple who are, in
   the end, the pool.

The inheritance, stated plainly: **the institutions that retired
from the risk left the private person with the risk and the fee** —
the container's fee, the advisor's fee, the annuity's loading — and
the whole of the corpus is the answer: the index as the pool the
household can run itself, the schedule as the time it can guarantee
itself, the written plan as the calm it must manufacture, the
arithmetic of the machines as the map of the weather. The machine is
no longer run for you. The manual is this.

## The deep lesson

Strip the vocabulary away and the note compresses to one fact, and
one distinction, and one inheritance. The fact: **a life is a
stream, and the institutions of this note are the two machines for
pricing and sharing the stream** — the pool, which spreads one
person's long tail across a population (the law of large numbers,
the index's averaging), and the time, which commits the money to
the stream's length (the compounding, the investing note's engine,
at the scale of a century of lives). Everything else — the defined
benefit, the
defined contribution, the annuity, the premium, the funding gap,
the national machine — is the fact applied to one of the stream's
two tails: the long tail (living too long, the stream outlasting
the money) and the short tail (dying or breaking too soon, the
money outlasting the stream, with the dependants' stream to fund in
between).

The distinction, which is the one the whole edifice stands on —
the one the bonds note taught: **the promise and the price are
different objects, and the century's whole funding story is the two
moving apart.** The promise — the stream, the schedule, the who —
is what was agreed, and it is stable; the price — the present
value, the funding gap, the budget line — is the market's valuation
of the stream, and it moves every day on the curve's weather, and
in the hard year the gap that appears in the public record is the
gap between the two objects, and the argument that follows is about
the price of a promise everyone still means to keep. Hold the
distinction and the news stops being a crisis: the deficit is a
rate, the surplus is a rate, and the promise, before the
discounting, is the simple stream — how many years, of what income,
for whom, beginning when — what the picture section protects, floor
and tail, from the weather.

The inheritance, which closes the note and hands it back to the
reader: the machines are machines for the average life, and they
work, on average, for the average life — the steady career, the
steady contributions, the average health, the mortality that tracks
the table. They fail, at the margins, for the people the average
does not describe: the interrupted career, the long-lived outlier,
the family that inherited a pot built on one market and must spend
in another, the household that needs the short tail in the year it
arrives. The institutions price the average, and the private person
*is* a margin — a single life, outside the pool's resolution, whose
stream is not the table's stream — and the defense the corpus has
been building is the defense of the margin: the plan written in the
calm, the floor on the promise that cannot fall, the tail on the
asset that can, the schedule that runs on the days that look like
no news, the machines' arithmetic known well enough that the
weather, when it comes, is recognized for what it is: a price,
moving, on a promise that is not moving — a promise the plan was
built to keep.

> You will age. Most people will need income in their seventies.
> The machines of this note exist because those two sentences are
> the only facts in finance that are certain, and the certainty is
> what makes them a business, and a study, and, for the private
> person who runs them alone, a plan.
