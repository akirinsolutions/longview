# The Mechanics of Investing
~ field: Investing
~ summary: Compounding, diversification, cost, and time — the four forces that decide most long-term outcomes, in that order of importance.

## Compounding: the engine

Everything in long-term investing is built on one mechanical fact:
**returns earn returns.** A 7% annual return is unremarkable in any
single year — it barely registers against a bad news cycle — and
over forty years it turns 1 into 14, because each year's 7% is
earned on a base that includes every previous year's 7%. The curve
is flat where you can see it and vertical where you cannot, which
is precisely why the effect is invisible to the eye that checks it
annually and decisive to the arithmetic that never looks.

The numbers, stated once, so the reader carries them:

- 7% a year doubles money in about ten years. Three doublings in
  thirty; five in fifty. A forty-year accumulation at 7% ends at
  roughly fourteen times the starting point, and *most* of that
  fourteen arrives in the last third of the period.
- The sensitivity is brutal at both ends: 5% and 9% look like a
  modest gap in any year; over thirty years they are the difference
  between 4.3 and 13.4 times the starting point. **The last few
  percentage points of return are worth more than the first few**,
  because they compound on the largest base.
- The time is the real variable. Ten years of compounding at 7% is
  1.96; twenty is 3.9; thirty is 7.6; forty is 14.8. The curve's
  second half is not a continuation of its first half; it is a
  different object. This is why the single most valuable investing
  act available to most people is not a better choice but an
  **earlier start** — the decade you add is worth more than any
  skill you could bring to the decade you have.

The corollary, in the painful direction, because the engine has a
second property and it is the one the bad years exploit:
compounding is **symmetric in time, not in money**. A loss of 50%
requires a gain of 100% to recover; a loss of 90% requires a gain
of 900%. The downside does not just subtract; it *extends the time*
the engine needs, and the time is the thing that cannot be bought
back. A portfolio that loses a third in a bad year does not need
thirty-three percent back, it needs fifty — and the fifty has to be
earned on the smaller base, at the same rate, in the same weather.
This asymmetry is the mathematical root of every risk rule in this
note: **protect the base, because the base is what the engine runs
on**, and a damaged engine needs more years to reach the same
destination, and the years are the one input the plan cannot
restore.

The engine's speed has a name in the field, and the name is worth
learning because it converts the percentage into a time: the rule of
seventy-two. A growing quantity doubles in roughly seventy-two
divided by its annual percentage — seven percent gives a little over
ten years, six gives a dozen, nine gives eight. The rule is an
approximation, but the approximation is the point, because it makes
the sensitivity *visible* to the eye: the difference between a six-
percent and a nine-percent portfolio is not a few percentage points
a year, it is the difference between doubling in twelve years and
doubling in eight, and over a forty-year arc that gap is the
difference between four doublings and five — half the distance to the
destination, lost or gained on a number that looks trivial in any
single year. The rule also exposes why the last few points of return
are worth so much to the owner of a large base: at the top of the
curve the base is so large that a one-point improvement is not a
one-percent improvement, it is a percentage of the largest number
the machine has ever carried. The arithmetic is the same arithmetic
the cost section will run again — the curve's shape multiplies
everything at the end, and it multiplies the good and the bad alike,
hardest exactly where the plan has least time to forgive it.

> A portfolio is not a pile of money. It is a machine with a
> memory, and the memory is the compounding. Damage the machine in
> year ten and you have damaged every year after.

## The shape of the curve: why the end of the arc dominates

The numbers above hide a fact that the graduate version of the
subject cannot afford to leave implicit, because it explains where
the real decisions live. The compounding curve is not uniform in
its *contribution*: the first half of the period builds the base,
and the second half, running on the base the first half built,
produces the greater part of the result. A forty-year accumulation
at seven percent ends at roughly fourteen times the start, and of
that fourteen, most of it — the greater share, and the share that
separates the outcomes — is produced in the last third of the
period, on the largest base, at the same unremarkable rate.

The practical consequence is a relocation of the entire subject.
The years that decide the result are not the years of starting,
which the reader can control from a distance; they are the **years
of ending**, which are the years the reader is *in* — the last
decade of the accumulation, the rollover into payout, the years in
which the base is largest, the damage is most expensive, and the
decisions are most consequential. The loss-asymmetry above, read
on the shape of the curve, becomes the whole warning in one
sentence: **a bad decision in the last third of the arc can
consume the first two thirds of it**, because the last third is
where the engine is heaviest and the asymmetry bites hardest. The
investor who loses a fifth in year thirty-five is not behind by a
fifth; they are behind by the time it will take to climb back, and
the climb is on the largest base the machine has ever carried,
which is to say, at the most expensive rate of asymmetry the
curve has.

This is also why the note's remaining sections read as they do.
The forces that decide the outcome — the allocation, the cost, the
behavior — are not evenly dangerous across the arc. They are
dangerous *where the curve is steep*, which is the end. The plan
that is built for the flat years and unprepared for the steep
years is the plan that fails exactly where the failure is most
expensive, and the behavior note's whole architecture — the plan
written in calm, the automation, the environment — is, in the end,
a set of defenses positioned at the end of this curve, because
that is where the machine is heaviest, and that is where the
weather is loudest, and those are the two facts that put the
reader, in their own last decade of accumulation, in the most
consequential hour of the subject.

## Diversification: the only free lunch

The second force is the one with a famous name and a quiet proof.
**Diversification** — holding many assets whose returns do not move
in lockstep — is the one risk reduction in finance that costs
nothing, because it does not lower the *expected* return the way
selling the best ideas would; it lowers the *variance*, the
spreads, the chance of the tail event. The reason is a simple
property of averaging: the average of many weakly-correlated bets
is less variable than any of them, even when every one of them is
individually uncertain. The portfolio's risk falls as the number of
uncorrelated pieces rises, and it falls toward a floor that is the
risk of the *world*, not of any one thing in it.

The proof, in the honest form: each new piece added to the
portfolio carries its own risk, but the *combination* of two
pieces that do not move together is less variable than either,
because the years one is bad are, partly, the years the other is
not, and the averages smooth each other. The effect is real from
the first few pieces — the jump from one holding to a handful is
the largest single risk reduction available anywhere in the
subject — and it continues, with diminishing returns, toward the
floor: the first ten companies remove most of the single-company
risk, the first ten markets remove most of the single-country risk,
and the pieces added after that buy less and less, because what is
left is the risk all the pieces share, which is the floor the next
section names.

The practical structure, in the order the diversification is
earned:

1. **Within an asset class** — many companies, not one. This is the
   cheap, mechanical kind; an index fund does it for a basis point
   of a fee, and it removes the risk that *the company* was wrong
   (the idiosyncratic risk, which is most of the risk of a single
   holding, and the one that is cheapest to eliminate, which is
   why eliminating it is the first act of any plan).
2. **Across asset classes** — stocks, bonds, the pieces that move
   for different reasons. This removes the risk that *one kind of
   weather* — a growth scare, an inflation shock — wiped the whole
   portfolio, because the pieces do not all suffer the same
   weather; the bonds note and the equities note are the two
   halves of this section, and the 2022 episode is the caution
   attached to it, because in a price shock the two halves can
   suffer the same weather for once.
3. **Across geographies** — the markets of many economies, which
   do not rise and fall together, even when they sometimes do. This
   removes the risk that *your country's* cycle was the bad one for
   the next decade, which is a risk the single-country saver
   carries without having chosen it.
4. **Across time** — the contribution, spread over years, so that
   the *entry price* is averaged rather than chosen. This is the
   diversification that matters most for the accumulator: it
   removes the risk that the single worst year to start was *your*
   year, and it is the only one of the four that the contributor
   controls directly, day by day, for the length of the arc.

The floor, stated honestly, because the free lunch has a limit:
diversification removes the risk of *things*, not the risk of
*markets*. The world-risk — the day when every asset class falls
together, because the fall is in the *price of time and the price
of risk itself* — cannot be diversified away; it can only be sized
for. The portfolio's irreducible risk is the risk of the economic
system it sits inside, and the mature investor's job is to hold
enough pieces that the *chosen* risks are small and the
*unchoosable* one is sized to be survivable. The free lunch is
real, and it is finite, and knowing where it ends is the difference
between the diversified portfolio and the portfolio that believes
it is diversified because it holds many pieces of the same weather.

## Cost: the silent tax

The third force is the least dramatic and the most certain: **the
cost of owning the portfolio, charged every year, for as long as
the portfolio exists.** The fee is a percentage of the base, so —
by the same compounding that builds the wealth — it compounds
against it, and it compounds *on the largest part of the base
first*, because the base is largest at the end, and the curve's
shape above means the tax is levied, year by year, on exactly the
part of the machine that is doing the heaviest work.

The arithmetic, because the percentages look too small to matter
and do: a portfolio that would return 7% gross, over thirty years,
net of a 1% annual cost returns 6% — and 6% to the thirtyeth power
is half of 7% to the thirtyeth power. A 1% fee is not 1% of the
final answer; it is **about 30% of the final answer**, quietly,
every year, for thirty years, with no bad-news headline to mark the
day it happened, because there is no day: the tax is a continuous
evaporation, and the continuous is the part the eye cannot see,
which is how the industry has lived on it for a century. The
comparison that the industry does not print: the difference
between a 0.2% index and a 1.5% active fund, over a working life,
is larger than the difference between the best active manager and
the worst — because the fee is *certain* and the manager's edge is
not, and the certain cost against the uncertain edge is a trade
the arithmetic has already decided, before the manager has done
anything.

The cost has three named layers, and the label on the fund covers
only the first:

- **The management fee** — the advertised number; the pay for the
  people who run it.
- **The transaction and structure costs** — the trading, the
  custody, the fund-within-fund layers; the number that lives in the
  fine print and shows up only in the *net* figures, which is where
  the comparison that matters is done.
- **The tax and drag costs** — the structure's inefficiency: the
  trading that triggers taxes, the tracking of an index that is
  hard to hold, the currency, the timing. The layer that no label
  shows at all, and the one that differs most between structures
  that carry the same management fee.

The practical rule that falls out of the arithmetic: **the cost is
the one input in the equation you fully control**, and the
compounding means that controlling it is worth more than any other
single act of skill available to a private investor. The index,
bought cheap, held long, is not a boring answer; it is the answer
that leaves the two forces you cannot control — the market and the
time — to do their work without a tax on the way, and it is the
only answer in the subject that is *certainly* what it claims to
be, which is a rarer property in this field than it has any right
to be.

The cost and the curve's shape meet in a way the single-year
percentage hides. Because the fee is charged on the base, and the
base is largest in the final third of the arc, the fee's damage is
*back-loaded*: the fee levied in year forty falls on a base many
times the size of the base in year one, and the fee's compounding
cost is therefore concentrated, year by year, in exactly the years
the curve's shape section said are the years that decide the result.
The two sections of this note are the same fact seen from two sides
— the curve makes the end expensive, and the tax is levied on the
end. This is why a fee that looks trivial in any single year — one
percent is a number the eye shrugs at — is in fact a structural tax
on the most valuable part of the machine, and why the index's
near-zero fee is not a minor saving: it is the decision to leave the
end of the arc untaxed. The investor who compares funds on a single
year's gross return is comparing them on the part of the machine
that matters least; the comparison that matters is the net figure,
held to the end of the arc, and the end is where the difference
between the funds becomes a chasm, and where the century of the
industry's living on the invisible tax was actually done.

## Timing: the force most people fight

The fourth force is the one investors lose to most often, and the
loss is *predictable*, which is what makes it the study: **the
attempt to be out of the market when it falls and in when it
rises.** The mechanics of the loss are few and stable:

- **The fall and the recovery are one event.** The market's bad
  years are followed, in the same move, by its good years; the
  person who sold into the fall to avoid the 30% decline has, in
  the same transaction, sold out of the 40% rebound, because the
  rebound is not a later event — it is the *other half* of the
  same event, arriving on a schedule the seller cannot see, and
  arriving, characteristically, soon enough that the seller has not
  yet calmed down when it does.
- **The two decisions must both be right.** Timing is a *pair* of
  bets — out before the fall, back before the rise — and the
  probability of both, in the right order, in the years that
  matter, is low enough that the strategy's long-run result is
  below the strategy of *staying in*, which only has to be right
  about the decade, and the decade is the unit the curve above
  shows the money is actually working in.
- **The behavioral tax** — the timing decision is made in the worst
  weather for judgment, when the portfolio is red and the news is
  loud, and the *re-entry* decision is made when the fear is still
  present and the rise has already started. The decision is
  structurally delayed in both directions, which is the reason the
  timed portfolio underperforms the held one by a margin that shows
  up, in the data, as a steady, compounding drag — the same shape
  as the fee drag, paid in missed rebounds, and the same reason it
  is invisible: it is the compounding of an absence, and absences
  do not send invoices.

The honest framing, because the reader will be tempted regardless:
**you cannot time the market, and you do not need to; you need to
size it.** The position that survives the worst plausible year —
the allocation that can fall 40% without forcing a sale, because
the sale is what converts a paper loss into a real one — is the
timed portfolio's only real competitor, and it wins on the simple
ground of having one decision instead of two, and making that one
decision in calm instead of in the weather. The timing attempt is
the force most people *fight*; the sizing is the force most
people *ignore*, and the evidence, in the form of the gap between
the market's return and the investor's return, is the behavior
note's central finding, which this note's arithmetic explains:
the gap is the compounding of the missed rebounds, and the missed
rebounds are the structural delay, and the structural delay is
the storm state of the self, deciding in the hour it was not built
for.

One more movement of the timing trap deserves naming, because it
happens *before* the crash rather than inside it: **the cash on the
sidelines** — the investor who waits for a better entry, holding
money in the account, watching the market, deciding that the rise
already happening is not a rise they will join, because they missed
its first part. The arithmetic is the asymmetry section's, run
against the plan: the money that waits for the correction is money
whose engine is off, and the correction, when it comes, comes with
the rebound, so the waiter who finally enters after the fall has
usually entered after the rebound has already begun — the same
structural delay the section described, reached from the other side
of the door. The market's correction is the waiter's only friend,
and the waiter cannot tell the correction from the start of a new
rise, because the two are indistinguishable in the hour they
arrive, and the hour is the hour the storm state is running the
decision. The defense is the section's own conclusion applied to
the quiet year: the contribution runs on the schedule whether or
not the market has fallen, because the schedule is the average, and
the average is the only entry the arithmetic can defend, and the
average is bought on days that look like no news at all — which is
to say, on ordinary days, on the days the storm state is asleep and
the plan is allowed to work.

## The allocation: deciding the risk in advance

Of the four forces, the one the private person most often
misunderstands is the allocation, because it is the only one that
looks like a *choice* while being, in effect, a *decision about
one's own nervous system*. The allocation — the mix of pieces in
the portfolio, the weight of the risk-taker (the equities note)
against the weather-shelter (the bonds note) — sets two numbers at
once, and the two numbers are the whole of the plan:

- **The survival number** — the worst plausible fall, in the worst
  plausible year, in the mix as chosen. The allocation that can
  fall forty percent without forcing a sale is the allocation the
  plan requires, because the fall is what the behavior note
  described as the moment the defenses are tested, and the test
  has to be survivable *in the fall*, not merely survivable in the
  after.
- **The ceiling number** — the return the mix can give in the good
  decades, which is lower than the all-risk mix and higher than
  the all-shelter mix, and which is the price paid for the
  survival: the allocation is a trade between the two numbers, and
  the trade is the plan, written in weights.

The honest version of the decision, because the popular version
falsifies it: the allocation is not a *prediction*, and it is not
a *personality test* (the quiz that asks how you feel about risk
is asking the storm state about a decision the calm state has to
make, which is the behavior note's error, with a form attached).
It is a **decision about the years you will live through**: the
horizon (the curve's length — the longer the arc, the more of the
mix the horizon can carry, because the steep part of the curve is
further away), the cash-flow needs (the bonds note's matching —
the money that is needed in five years is not money the allocation
is allowed to lose in five years), and the one variable the reader
can actually control in the bad year, which is the mix itself,
decided in calm, in writing, in advance. The allocation, in other
words, is the plan made into a number, and the number is the thing
the storm self inherits, and the inheritance is the precommitment
working as designed.

## The contribution: the accumulator's real instrument

The force the summary line names as time, and the force the
contribution schedule actually *is*: **the contribution, spread
over the arc, is the private person's one continuous instrument**,
and it deserves its own section because it is the piece of the plan
the reader touches every month, and the piece most likely to be
broken, because it is the piece the weather touches.

The mechanism is the fourth level of diversification above, made
into a habit: the contribution buys at whatever price the market
is, on a schedule, for the length of the arc, so that the *entry*
is averaged over years rather than chosen at a moment. The
contribution is therefore the accumulator's diversification across
time, and its virtue is not that it finds the bottom (it does not;
it finds the *average*), but that it removes from the reader the
one decision the reader is least able to make — *when to start,
when to buy, when to stop* — and replaces it with a rule that runs
in the background, buying the rise and the fall alike, at the
schedule's price, which is the market's average price, and the
average is, over the arc, the only entry the arithmetic can
defend.

The danger of the instrument is the one the behavior note names,
and it is worth naming here, in this note's section, where the plan
lives: **the contribution is the first thing the storm state
pauses.** After the bad year, the red portfolio, the loud news,
the paused contribution — the pause that costs more than the pause
itself, because the paused year is a year of the arc, and the arc
is the engine, and the engine running with no fuel for a year is
the asymmetry of the curve above, working against the plan: the
years the contribution stops are the years the base stops growing,
and the growth stops at exactly the moment the machine most needs
to keep running. The defense is the one the behavior note
engineered — the contribution on a schedule, automated, invisible,
a background process rather than a decision — and the reason it is
the first defense to build is that it is the one the storm state
can actually break, and it is the one the plan cannot afford to
lose, because the contribution is the fuel, and the fuel is the
time, and the time is the whole subject.

## The complete picture: what actually decides the outcome

The four forces, in the order they decide a private investor's
forty-year result, which is the order the evidence keeps giving:

1. **Time in the market** — the length of the compounding, which
   dwarfs every other term; the early start, the late stop, the
   uninterrupted middle, and the contribution that keeps the fuel
   flowing across it.
2. **The allocation** — the mix of pieces, which sets the
   portfolio's risk and therefore its survival through the bad
   decade, and its return ceiling in the good one; the plan made
   into a number, decided in calm.
3. **The cost** — the certain, compounding drag, controllable to a
   basis point or a percentage point, worth the difference either
   way, and the one force in the equation that is *certain* in a
   field of uncertainty, which is why its control is worth more
   than the skill.
4. **The timing and the behavior** — the uncertain, self-inflicted
   drag, the one that varies by temperament, and the one this
   centre's final note is about, which is to say, the one the
   reader is about to read, and the one the plan must be built
   against, because it is the only force of the four that is
   *inside the reader* rather than around it.

The returns of the market itself — the big, public, argued-over
number — are *not on the list*, because they are the one term the
investor does not choose: everyone who stayed in got them, and the
differences between investors' forty-year outcomes are explained,
overwhelmingly, by the four forces above, which are all *within the
investor's own hands*. The market pays everyone the same; the
mechanics sort them. The reader who carries this list has, in
effect, the whole subject: the engine, the shape of the curve, the
free lunch and its floor, the silent tax, the force most people
fight, the decision made in advance, and the fuel that keeps the
engine running — ten sections of the corpus, in the end, all
pointing at the same four numbers, and the four numbers all
controllable, and the controllability is the point the whole centre
exists to make.

> The market is the river. The forces above are the boat. The
> river is the same for everyone who floats in it, and the boats
> are not.

## The private person's plan

The synthesis, because this is the note's purpose and the rest of
the material exists to serve it. The plan is one page, and the page
has the four dials on it, in the order of the complete picture:

1. **The time** — the arc, written down: the start (today, because
   the curve's shape makes today the cheapest start available),
   the horizon (the rollover, the need, the life the money is for),
   and the one rule that protects the arc — the contribution is
   uninterrupted, and the pause is the decision that needs a
   written reason, not a mood.
2. **The allocation** — the mix, written down, with the survival
   number attached: the worst plausible fall of the mix, and the
   acceptance of it, *in writing, in calm*, so that the bad year
   meets a decision that was already made. The mix is reviewed on
   a calendar, not on a headline — the behavior note's schedule,
   applied to the dial.
3. **The cost** — the structure, written down: the index, the low
   fee, the simple wrapper, the one that is certainly what it
   claims to be. The cost dial is set once and left, because the
   arithmetic has already decided it, and because the dial that is
   decided in calm and left in place is the dial the storm state
   cannot touch.
4. **The behavior** — the defenses, written down, in the if-then
   form the behavior note gave them: the bad year, the fall of
   thirty percent, the contribution continues, nothing is sold, the
   plan is read, the screen is closed. The page that exists is the
   self that is present in the crash week.

The page, held, is the whole of the subject made portable: the
engine and its shape, the free lunch and its floor, the tax and
its certainty, the weather and its schedule, and the one variable
in the equation that is the reader. The market is the river, and
the river is the same for everyone who floats in it; the page is

## The deep lesson

Strip the vocabulary away and the note compresses to one fact,
stated without the arithmetic: **the outcome of a financial life
is decided, mostly, by the things about the financial life that are
boring** — the years of the arc, the pieces of the mix, the cost
of the wrapper, the pauses of the contribution — and decided,
mostly, *before the interesting years arrive*, because the boring
things are the things that can be decided in calm, and the
interesting years are the years the calm is not available, and the
plan is the calm, saved.

The final inversion, which closes the note and opens the one that
follows it: the mechanics are the *map* of the forces, and the map
is complete the moment the reader can name them — the engine, the
curve, the lunch, the tax, the fight, the decision, the fuel —
but the map is not the *driver*, and the driver is the reader, in
the years that the map describes, making the boring things happen,
in the weather that the map predicts, with the calm that the map
assumes. The map tells the reader what the forces are; the reader
decides, in the end, which of the forces they will let decide
their life.
