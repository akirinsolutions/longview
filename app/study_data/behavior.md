# Why People Lose Money
~ field: Behavior
~ summary: Most investment losses are not made by the market; they are made by the investor, in the moments the market's noise meets the mind's shortcuts.

## The quiet finding

The central empirical fact of the last thirty years of finance is
uncomfortable and stable: **most of the gap between the market's
return and the investor's return is self-inflicted.** The market
pays what it pays; the fund's shareholder, on average, captures less
of it — persistently, across asset classes, across countries, across
decades — and the shortfall shows up in the same shape everywhere:
investors buy after the rises and sell after the falls, so that the
money enters high and leaves low, and the portfolio that *stays*
outperforms the portfolio that *reacts*.

The size of the gap is not small. In the classic studies of fund
investors it runs to a percentage point or more a year — a number
that looks trivial in a single year and, by the compounding
arithmetic of the investing note, resolves over a working life into
the difference between the top and the bottom of the outcomes
distribution. A one-point annual gap, compounded for thirty years,
is the difference between a portfolio of roughly eight times the
starting point and one of roughly five. The whole note is about why
a gap that small *in* the year ends up that large *over* the
period.

Who bears the gap, and who should worry. The classic studies follow
*retirement savers* — the steady contributors with no intention of
trading at all, the very person the investing note describes — and
the shortfall appears for exactly that person: money that entered
after the rise, or left after the fall, without a single wrong
decision in the investor's own eyes. The studies also carry honest
caveats, and the graduate reader holds them. The timing data is
partly self-reported, the estimates are noisy, and part of the
shortfall is fee drag, which the investing note's cost section
already counts. None of the caveats shrinks the finding. The fee is
a number the industry chooses, and its size is bounded; the timing
is a number the mind chooses, and it is the larger of the two — the
larger, and the one this note can act on.

And the source of the gap is not information, not fees, not luck —
the cost force of the investing note covers the fee, and this note
covers the rest. It is **behavior**: the way the mind actually
decides, under stakes, under noise, and under time pressure,
differs from the way the rational model of investing assumes it
does. The market is not the cause of most losses; the market is the
*weather*. One more feature of the finding deserves naming, because
it converts the finding from a joke into an engineering problem:
the gap is *stable*. The same investors, in the same markets, over
the same decades, repeat the same pattern — the buy after the rise,
the sale after the fall — with a gap that holds its size year after
year. A loss that is self-inflicted and stable is not a character
flaw and it is not bad luck; it is a *design* failure, and design
failures are the one kind of failure that design can fix. The rest
of this note is a map of the difference between the mind and the
model, and of what to do with the map.

## The mind's shortcuts, and where they bite

The mind decides with **heuristics** — fast, frugal rules of thumb
that served humanity well for ten thousand years of small stakes and
become expensive at the scale of a portfolio. The ones that matter
here, each with its financial signature:

- **Recency** — the mind estimates the future from the recent past,
  because the recent past is what is available. After a bad year,
  the available evidence says *markets fall*; after a good decade,
  it says *markets rise forever*. The recency shortcut is why risk
  aversion is highest exactly after the risk has been paid — the
  investor who just watched a thirty-percent fall has just been
  *shown* what risk looks like, and prices the next decade on the
  last year's footage.
- **Salience** — the mind weighs what is vivid over what is
  frequent. A crash is vivid; a boring decade of seven percent a
  year is not. The portfolio's risk is therefore experienced in its
  tails and ignored in its middle, which is the opposite of where
  the long-run return lives.
- **Narrative** — the mind converts numbers into stories, because
  stories are the native format of human understanding. Every
  market move gets a story (the oil, the election, the genius
  trader), and a good story *feels like a cause*, which closes the
  inquiry. The market, which is a collision of ten thousand motives
  (the markets note), rarely has one story in it, and the investor
  who finds the story has, in the finding, stopped looking.
- **The status quo** — the mind treats the current state as the
  default and prices change as loss. An investor who holds is not
  deciding to hold each year; they are *not deciding to sell*, and
  the not-deciding costs nothing in feeling while it compounds in
  money. The same shortcut, pointed the other way, is why people
  hold losing positions — selling is a *change*, and the status quo
  is the default.

The common shape of the four, which the graduate version names:
each shortcut is an economy of *attention*. The mind runs on a
budget — it cannot hold forty years of evidence, ten thousand
counterparties, and a moving risk model in working memory — so it
samples. It samples the recent (recency), the vivid (salience), the
explainable (narrative), and the unchanged (status quo). Every
sample is a reasonable proxy for the truth in the world it was
tuned for; and every proxy is a distortion in the financial world,
where the stakes are large, the feedback is slow, and the truth is
a *distribution over decades* that no single year of samples can
represent. The tragedy is not that the mind is defective; it is
that the shortcuts were tuned for a world in which a bad decision
cost a day's food, not a career's savings. The mind is doing
exactly what ten thousand years of evolution asked of it. Finance
simply changed the stakes without changing the mind.

The origin of the budget deserves a word, because it explains why
the shortcuts cannot be argued away. The attention economy above is
not a defect the mind shipped; it is the cost of a machine that runs
on a small supply of energy in a world where evidence arrived
*slowly* — a season of it, not a feed of it. When evidence arrived
slowly, sampling the recent and the vivid was almost correct: the
recent was the best proxy for the coming, and the vivid was usually
the dangerous. Finance is the first environment in the machine's
history in which evidence arrives fast *and* wrong: the feed is the
entire market, re-estimated every minute, and the recent sample is
now a sample of noise, not of a slow world. The shortcuts are doing
exactly what they were built for, in a world that no longer exists.
You cannot correct a calibration error with effort in the moment;
you can only move the decision out of the miscalibrated hour.

## The named biases, in the order they hurt

The profession has a vocabulary for the shortcuts, and the five that
do the most damage to real portfolios, in observed order:

1. **Loss aversion** — a loss of a given size *hurts roughly twice
   as much* as an equal gain pleases, in the felt experience. The
   consequence for a portfolio is structural: the investor who
   feels losses double will, feeling rationally and acting
   irrationally, cut the position that falls — cutting exactly the
   position whose fall is the price of its future rise, and
   holding the one that rises, which becomes, over time, the
   position that falls. The portfolio is silently re-allocated,
   every bad week, toward the assets that have just underperformed
   *in feeling*.
2. **The disposition effect** — the measured face of loss
   aversion: people sell their winners and keep their losers,
   because selling the winner *banks* the pleasure and selling the
   loser *realizes* the pain. The tax is double: the kept loser is
   the one most likely to keep falling (the reason it fell), and
   the sold winner is the one most likely to keep rising (the
   reason it rose). The portfolio ends up holding its failures and
   having sold its successes — a slow, compounding inversion of the
   one rule that works.
3. **Overconfidence** — the mind overestimates the skill in its own
   calls, especially after the calls that happened to win, because
   the wins are the ones it remembers. The overconfident investor
   trades more, leverages more, and concentrates more — the three
   acts that convert a good decade into a bad portfolio. The data's
   quiet joke: the most active traders are the worst performers,
   and their activity is the overconfidence made visible.
4. **Herd and momentum-chasing** — the mind reads the crowd's
   movement as information, because for most of history the crowd's
   movement *was* information (the herd moves because the herd
   knows). In markets the signal is noisier than that, and the
   chase buys the top of the crowd's own movement — the peak that
   exists because the crowd built it — with the same confidence the
   crowd gave it.
5. **Mental accounting and regret** — the mind keeps separate
   ledgers for money that should be fungible: the salary, the house
   fund, the lucky win, and each ledger has its own rules and its
   own emotional charge. The win that arrived by luck is "free
   money" to spend recklessly; the salary is "safe money" to park.
   The same dollar, differently labeled, spends differently — and
   the label is chosen by the story, not by the arithmetic. The
   companion bias is **regret aversion**: the mind prices the pain
   of *choosing wrongly* — the fund that missed the rise, the house
   that was not bought — more heavily than the pain of the outcome
   itself, because a made choice haunts while a fate does not. Its
   practical signature is the sale that is not made: holding the
   falling position is not conviction, it is the avoidance of the
   grief of confirming the error. The account stays open, the regret
   stays imaginary, and the imaginary regret is the one that costs.

The list is a map of the same terrain: **the mind experiences
investing as a sequence of wins and losses to be judged one at a
time, while the portfolio's truth is a single forty-year arc to be
judged only in the aggregate.** Every bias above is the gap between
those two units of accounting, and the entire practice of
behavioral investing is the attempt to make the investor's unit of
accounting match the portfolio's.

> The market transfers wealth from the impatient to the patient.
> The biases above are the impatience, itemized.

## The reference point: where the pain actually lives

The four biases share one deeper mechanism, and naming it is the
graduate step: **the mind does not value outcomes, it values
changes** — and the change is measured against a *reference point*
that the investor did not choose and cannot see.

The unit of feeling in the portfolio is not the wealth level but
the *difference from the reference*: the purchase price, last
year's value, the peak, the neighbor's return. The same portfolio
can be described as "up ten percent this year" or "down three
percent from its high," and the two descriptions are the identical
portfolio, with a different reference point, and they produce
different decisions. The reference point is not a neutral marker;
it is the zero of the mind's pain-and-pleasure scale, and it
*drifts*: after a rise, the peak becomes the new reference, so a
portfolio that has never lost in absolute terms *feels* like it is
losing the moment it stops rising.

That drift is what makes the rise-then-fall sequence the most
dangerous sequence in finance. The rise to the peak produces
pleasure, and the peak quietly resets the reference; the fall is
then measured against the peak, not against the start, and the
loss-aversion weight — the twice-as-much fact above — applies to a
loss that is partly *imagined*, the part that never was lost. The
typical sequence of a bad year, read on this mechanism, runs like
this: a portfolio that rose hard, then fell back to where it began
a year earlier, *feels* like a total loss — because the reference
is the peak — and the sale that "makes sense" in that feeling is
the sale of a portfolio that, in absolute terms, did what it was
supposed to do. The investor who can see the reference point move
can see the sale for what it is: a decision made on a number that
moved, not on the money that is there.

One more movement of the reference deserves naming, because it is
the one that surprises the saver rather than the trader: **the
reference attaches to income, not only to wealth.** A pay rise is
experienced as a gain — last year's pay is the floor — and the
standard response is to spend the difference, so that the rise is
consumed rather than accumulated. The reverse move, the cut, is a
loss of roughly double the felt weight, and the cut that is absorbed
by spending less is absorbed *permanently*: the standard of living
re-sets to the lower income and does not rise back when the income
returns. The cost of living is thus, in the mind's accounting, a
one-way ratchet, and the ratchet is why a rise in wealth and a rise
in income produce different behavior even when the balance sheet
says the two are the same. The investor who sees the ratchet can
build the plan against it — the contribution that scales with
income, set before the income scales the spending.

The design consequence follows directly and is the hinge of the
note: **the reference point is the one variable in the decision
that the investor can actually choose** — by choosing what they
look at, and how often, and in what unit. The next sections are
that choice, engineered.

## The moments: where the losses are actually made

The biases do not hurt uniformly; they fire in **specific moments**,
and the moments are few, which is what makes them defensible:

- **The peak and the rally it starts** — the point of maximum
  optimism, maximum news, and maximum *felt* safety; the moment the
  herd's shortcut is loudest and the position is largest. Buying
  here is not a prediction error; it is a *weather* event the mind
  was not built to resist.
- **The crash and the week after** — the point of maximum fear,
  where loss aversion, recency, and salience all fire at once and
  the sale that "makes sense" is the sale that locks in the
  bottom. The decision that costs a career is usually made in this
  week, and made *feel* like prudence.
- **The boring middle years** — the long stretch where nothing
  dramatic happens and the compounding (the investing note's
  engine) does its quiet work; the moment the *status quo* shortcut
  either saves the portfolio (the holder who does nothing right) or
  quietly leaks it (the rebalancer who never rebalances, the
  contributor who pauses the contribution after one flat year).
- **The near-miss and the win** — the small win that feels like
  skill and buys the next overconfident position; the near-miss
  loss that feels like the market's malice and poisons the next
  decade's patience. The mind's scoreboard is wrong-sized for
  compounding: it keeps score in events, the portfolio in decades.
- **The rollover** — the hour the compounding machine of the
  investing note switches from *deposit* mode to *payout* mode: the
  retirement, the buy of the house, the year the savings must become
  an income. Every shortcut of this note fires at the rollover at
  once, in its most concentrated form: the recency is the final
  year's return, the reference is the peak of the accumulated sum,
  the loss aversion converts the *withdrawal* into a *loss* (taking
  one's own money out feels like parting with it), and the herd is
  the neighbor's retirement plan. The rollover is where the
  forty-year arc becomes the next thirty years of living on it, and
  the decision made there — what the payout rule is, what the mix
  does — is the largest behavioral decision of the financial life,
  because it is the one that converts the whole accumulated result
  into a stream of felt, yearly, reference-measured experience. The
  defense is the same architecture: the payout rule written in
  calm, the mix decided in calm, the first withdrawal on a
  schedule, rather than on the year's news.

The design implication falls out directly: **the decisions that
matter are made in the moments the mind is least able to make
them**, which is why the effective defense is not better thinking
*in* the moment — the moment wins, every time, because it is
present and the plan is abstract — but decisions made *before* the
moment, while the mind is calm: the written plan, the automatic
contribution, the pre-committed rule for the bad year, the
rebalance on a calendar rather than a headline. The defense is
**architecture, not willpower**: build the decision structure so
that the moments find a plan already in place instead of a mind
alone with the screen.

## Why willpower loses

The naive defense — *I will remember the plan in the crash week* —
fails for a structural reason, not a character reason, and the
reason is worth stating exactly. In the crash week, the brain that
has to execute the plan is a *different state* of the same person.
The salience system is saturated with the loss; the loss-aversion
weight is at its yearly peak; the news feed is narrating the fall
in the investor's own numbers; and against all of that, the plan
competes as an *abstract memory* — a document written in another
season by another, calmer state of the self. In that contest the
present always wins, because the felt is real-time and the planned
is remembered, and the mind's attention budget, already described,
spends on the real-time. No amount of resolution changes the
contest; the resolution is made in the calm state, and it is
*executed* by the storm state, and the two states do not share an
attention budget.

The economics has a name for this, and it is the cleanest statement
of the whole problem. A person who decides in two states — calm and
crisis — is a *principal and an agent at different times*: the
calm self makes commitments the crisis self has no reason to honor,
which is precisely the problem that contracts solve for strangers,
and that precommitment solves for yourself. Every defense in the
next section is one device in different forms — **binding the
crisis self to the calm self's decision before the crisis
arrives**, so that the crisis self, when it wakes, finds a
structure already in place instead of an open decision. The
engineering statement, which is the whole note in one sentence:
**the goal is not a braver investor; it is an investor whose
decisions are made at times when the investor is calm.** Move the
decision, not the weather. You cannot wait out the storm with your
armors on; you can only armor the decisions while it is quiet.

## The defenses, plainly

The working toolkit, in the order of reliability:

1. **Write the plan while calm.** One page: the allocation, the
   contribution, the rebalance rule, and — the piece most plans
   omit — *what you will do in the bad year, in advance, in
   sentences*. "If the portfolio falls thirty percent, I will
   contribute the same and sell nothing." The plan's job is to be
   the self that is present in the crash week, on the investor's
   behalf.
2. **Automate the accumulation.** The contribution on a schedule,
   the rebalance on a date, the buying *through* the noise.
   Automation is the only defense that does not depend on the
   investor showing up, and it converts the boring middle years —
   the compounding years — from a test of character into a
   background process.
3. **Size for the worst plausible year.** The allocation chosen so
   that the portfolio's worst realistic year is *survivable without
   acting* — because the act, in that year, is where the loss
   becomes real. The size is the precommitment; the survivability
   is the plan's acceptance test. A portfolio that can fall forty
   percent without forcing a sale is a portfolio the investor can
   keep.
4. **Keep the scoreboard honest.** Measure the portfolio against
   the *benchmark over the full arc*, not against last month, not
   against the neighbor, not against the year's news, not against
   the peak. The unit of account, made visible, is the unit of
   decision; a decade-scale scoreboard makes the decade-scale
   decision, and it does so by moving the reference point — the
   one variable the investor can choose — from the peak to the
   plan.
5. **Respect the moments.** Name them when they arrive — *this is
   the peak weather; this is the crash week* — because the named
   moment is a moment the plan already covers, and the covered
   moment is a moment the mind can let pass without deciding.
   Naming is the smallest act the storm self can do that counts as
   cooperation.
6. **Write the plan as if-then.** The research on precommitment has
   a precise finding: rules written as *if-then* commitments — if X
   happens, I will do Y, at time T, in place P — are kept at
   roughly twice the rate of abstract resolutions. The crash-week
   plan that says "I will remain calm" is an abstract resolution;
   the plan that says "if the portfolio is down fifteen percent, on
   the first Monday, I will deposit the monthly contribution and
   close the screen" is an if-then, and the if-then is the form
   the storm self can execute, because the *if* recognizes its own
   weather and the *then* has already been decided. The five
   defenses above all work best in this form.

## The environment: design the room, not the self

The defenses above are interior; the same logic applies to the
*room* — the environment in which the decisions happen — and the
room does as much to the decision as the self does. The person in
the storm is shaped by what is in front of them: the portfolio
page that updates every second, the news feed that narrates every
tick, the social network that broadcasts the winners and the
losers. Each of those is a *salience machine* built by other
people, running on the same shortcuts this note describes, and the
investor who builds their environment against them has done as much
for their own decision quality as any amount of introspection.

The practical list, in the order of leverage:

1. **Check on a schedule, not on a feed.** The portfolio is a slow
   object — a forty-year arc — and a slow object is watched on a
   slow clock. A weekly glance, on a set day, is the full
   monitoring the plan requires; the daily number is a number the
   plan never asked for.
2. **Remove the live display from the phone.** The number that is
   always there is the number that is always *felt*, and the felt
   is the reference point drifting — the mechanism from two
   sections back — running on a clock the investor cannot set. The
   same portfolio, invisible, is a quieter portfolio.
3. **Keep the plan where the login is.** The storm self meets
   whatever is nearest when it opens the account; if the plan's
   page is the thing it meets first, the storm self reads the calm
   self's sentences before it reads the red number.
4. **Choose the default.** The auto-contribution, the
   auto-rebalance, the provider whose home screen shows the
   ten-year line rather than the day. The default is the decision
   that the storm self inherits by default, and the inheritance is
   the precommitment working as designed.
5. **Let friction do the work.** The small cost of acting — a call
   to the advisor, a written note to the calm self, a twenty-four
   hour rule before any sale — is the tollbooth that stops the
   impulsive sale, and friction is the cheapest, most reliable
   friend the plan has. The storm self is impulsive; the impulse
   is short; a short delay is a long wall.

The deep point: **every financial product you touch is designed for
the storm self.** The industry's incentives run on attention and
action — the trading, the switching, the chasing — so the
environment you accept is, silently, an opponent in the game this
note describes. Choosing your environment is the first, and in
practice the most consequential, defense, because it is the only
one that works on the days the investor forgets everything else.

## Behavior at the scale of the corpus

Every other note in this corpus has a behavioral layer, and the
layer is where its warnings stop being about the market and start
being about the reader:

- **The money note's expectations.** The public's inflation
  expectations are the same recency-and-salience machinery at the
  scale of a nation: the wage-price spiral runs on what people
  *believe* prices will do, and the belief is fed by the vivid,
  recent price moves. When the public's expectation unanchors, the
  cure is credibility built in the calm years — because in the hot
  year, at the national scale as at the personal one, the mind is
  not listening.
- **The banking note's run.** The run is a coordination game
  *between* minds, and the guarantee works only because the minds
  it contains are each running the same recency calculation. The
  2023 run moved through a social network because the network is
  the fastest salience channel available; the defense that stopped
  it was the public instrument — the promise, honored in the hour —
  not any discipline of the depositors.
- **The bonds note's 2022.** The worst bond year in a generation
  hit a generation that had never lived through one: the holders
  were *calm-decade* holders, and when the price fell, the
  salience of the fall met the recency of the zero-rate years, and
  the liquidations that followed turned a price event into a loss
  event. The bonds note's lesson — that a bond's safety is a
  statement about its payments, not its price — is a lesson the
  mind can only use if it has been learned in advance, in calm,
  because in the falling year it sounds like a reassurance nobody
  believes.
- **The equities note's cycle.** The multiple looks cheap in the
  boom and expensive in the slump, and the mind, running on the
  multiple as its headline number, is structurally invited to sell
  in the trough and buy in the peak — which is why the cycle is
  the most counterintuitive fact in valuation, and the one the
  defenses must be built to *enforce* rather than to *understand*.
  Understanding the cycle, in a falling market, is exactly as
  useless as understanding the weather.

The unifying statement: **in the critical moments, the market's
noise and the mind's shortcuts are the same event.** The crash is
vivid to everyone at once; the recency is the crash for everyone
at once; the herd is the herd at once. The defense therefore
cannot be individual discipline — the weather is shared, and
discipline is not — it must be individual *architecture*: the plan,
the automation, the environment, the friction. The architecture is
the only part of the investor that does not feel the weather,
which is why it is the only part that can be trusted to act in it.

## The deep lesson

Strip the vocabulary away and the whole note compresses to one
fact, stated without the clinical terms: **people lose money the
way sailors lose ships — not in the calm, but in the one weather
they were not built for, at the one hour the training did not
reach.** The market is that weather. The compounding machine of the
investing note is the ship, and it is the same ship for everyone.
The differences in where people finish are explained, mostly, by
what they did in the storms — and the storms, as the map above
shows, arrive on a schedule the mind cannot predict but the
*structure* can plan for.

The scale of the lesson widens as the note ends. The same weather,
the same shortcuts, run on every scale at once — the household's,
the market's, the nation's. The public's inflation beliefs, the
depositor's queue, the calm-decade bondholder's memory: each is the
mind of one section of this corpus meeting the weather of another,
and the financial century, read at this zoom, is a history of
individual minds running the same program in the same weather at
the same time — the panic of the 1930s, the optimism of the 1990s,
the unbelief of 2008, the chasing of 2021. The private person's
defense cannot be against the crowd; the crowd is the market, and
the market is the weather. The private person's defense is against
the self, because the self is the one variable in the equation they
control, and the whole note has been the map of that single
variable.

The final inversion, which closes the note: the study of behavior
is not the study of a flaw to be fixed in the self. It is the study
of a **design problem** — how to build a financial life whose
critical decisions are made by the calm builder, executed by the
architecture, and merely *witnessed* by the person in the storm.
The self in the storm is not the investor; the plan is. And the
reason the inversion holds is the reason the whole note holds: the
mind is not a machine that can be repaired, it is a machine that
can only be *engineered around* — a machine that is, in everything
else, the instrument of the very life the plan exists to protect.
